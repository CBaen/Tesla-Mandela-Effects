## Hook Description
February 1882, Budapest City Park at sunset. A 26-year-old engineer walks with his friend, reciting Goethe. Mid-verse, he stops dead. Grabs a stick. Starts drawing frantically in the dirt—complete technical diagrams of a machine that doesn't exist yet. An induction motor powered by alternating current, with rotating magnetic fields, precise coil specifications, pole configurations, everything.

His friend Anital Szigety watches, stunned. This isn't someone figuring something out. This is someone remembering something. Complete, perfect, instant knowledge.

The engineer is Nikola Tesla. He will later file patents for this exact motor. No prototypes. No failed attempts. No iterative improvements. The first physical motor he builds works perfectly because he's not inventing it—he's copying it from a vision that felt like memory.

But here is the problem: in that vision, Tesla remembered building the motor with old hands. His hands at age 80. He experienced constructing it, testing it, knowing it worked—before he ever built it for the first time.

This is documented evidence of a temporal causality loop. The motor taught itself to Tesla.

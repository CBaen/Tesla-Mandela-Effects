Tesla's Journey: ## Motivation

By 1896, Tesla had achieved tremendous success with alternating current systems, but he was increasingly concerned about knowledge preservation. He'd witnessed how Edison's direct current advocates tried to suppress AC technology, how patents could be stolen, how scientific discoveries could be lost when laboratories burned or researchers died unexpectedly. His motivation for temporal transmission wasn't just scientific curiosity—it was creating an unbreakable backup system for critical knowledge. If he could send information backward through time to be preserved by ancient civilizations who wouldn't understand but would keep the artifacts, he could ensure his work survived regardless of what happened in his own era.

## Process

Tesla's temporal transmission research began in 1894 when he noticed that objects subjected to extremely high-frequency electromagnetic fields displayed temporal instability—brief moments where they seemed to exist slightly out of phase with present time. By 1895, he'd developed theoretical framework: if electromagnetic resonance could create localized spacetime distortions, objects could be displaced temporally. The challenge was directional control and preventing objects from being destroyed during transmission.

He spent 18 months (1895-1896) building the temporal displacement apparatus in his Houston Street laboratory. The device used multiple Tesla coils operating at precisely synchronized frequencies to create a resonance bubble where spacetime became malleable. Mathematical calculations allowed him to target specific historical coordinates based on astronomical positions and Earth's temporal location in spacetime.

For the test object, Tesla designed something brilliant: a functional device using only materials available in the target era (250 BCE), ensuring ancient peoples would preserve it. The electrochemical cell would work as a battery, giving it practical value. But he encoded his entire technical specifications in the physical structure—every dimension, every ratio, every material characteristic represented data. It was simultaneously primitive technology (ancient battery) and sophisticated information storage (binary-encoded technical library).

## Realization

On September 14, 1896, Tesla activated the displacement apparatus with the battery inside the resonance chamber. The device vanished cleanly—no explosion, no residue, just instantaneous absence. He'd succeeded in temporal transmission. But immediate realization hit: he had no way to verify the device arrived at target coordinates. Unlike his teleportation test (Kentucky Meat Shower, 1876) where news reports confirmed arrival, sending objects to 250 BCE meant waiting for archaeological discovery—potentially centuries in the future.

Worse: he realized temporal transmission was more dangerous than teleportation. Teleporting objects across space couldn't change history. Sending objects into the past could create paradoxes. What if his battery changed Parthian development? What if they figured out electricity 2,000 years early and altered human history?

He calculated the risks and concluded the battery was safe—too simple for Parthians to reverse-engineer electrical theory, but functional enough they'd preserve it. But the realization that temporal transmission could alter history made him cautious. He couldn't send major technologies backward. Only information storage.

His detailed documentation of excavation context—which layers, which associated pottery, precise depth—became crucial evidence that the battery was genuinely ancient despite modern manufacturing characteristics. Without his careful reporting, the temporal transmission evidence would be incomplete. His field notes, preserved in König's archives, were rediscovered in 2002 and provided key data for temporal analysis.

## Character Arc
Began as practical laborer who viewed archaeology as employment, not intellectual pursuit. Finding the battery forced him to recognize his role wasn't just economic—his documentation preserved crucial scientific evidence. Continued excavation work through 1950s, became known for meticulous recording of contextual information. His reputation grew as archaeologists recognized that local excavators' field observations were often more reliable than foreign archaeologists' interpretations. Died 1984, age 74, having trained next generation of Iraqi excavation specialists. His legacy: the local excavator whose careful field documentation proved Tesla's temporal transmission worked, though he never knew that's what he'd documented.

Tesla's Journey: ## Motivation

By 1917, Tesla's Wardenclyffe wireless power project had collapsed financially. J.P. Morgan and other investors had withdrawn support, convinced wireless power transmission was commercially impractical. But Tesla remained obsessed—he knew the physics worked, requiring only sufficient scale and proper geographic positioning to prove it. His motivation wasn't commercial anymore; it was legacy. He needed to demonstrate wireless power transmission across oceanic distances before he died, even if he had to do it secretly.

## Process

Tesla spent 1915-1916 analyzing global geomagnetic data, seeking remote locations with optimal electromagnetic properties for receiver stations. Easter Island emerged as ideal: volcanic basalt with excellent dielectric properties, positioned on Pacific geomagnetic field lines, isolated enough to prevent interference from industrial electrical systems, and culturally protected by indigenous traditions that would discourage future investigation. He secured covert naval funding by emphasizing communication applications while privately focusing on power reception.

The installation process (April-December 1917) required disguising electromagnetic infrastructure as archaeology. Tesla designed each Moai as a functional antenna tower: volcanic basalt bodies carved with specific geometries for electromagnetic resonance, buried Tesla coils providing induction capabilities, copper cables networking the statues into a synchronized array. He positioned statues using geomagnetic calculations disguised as cultural site selection. The Rapa Nui people watched but interpreted construction through their cultural framework—Tesla encouraged this, knowing indigenous traditions would protect his installation better than any security system.

## Realization

By 1920, Tesla realized the Easter Island array successfully received wireless power transmissions across 8,000+ miles of ocean—but at efficiency levels too low for practical application. He could demonstrate the physics worked, but scaling to useful power levels would require thousands of receiver stations globally and transmitter power levels that were economically impossible with 1920s technology. His dream of wireless power replacing wired electrical grids was theoretically sound but practically unachievable.

January seventh, 1943. Room 3327. Hotel New Yorker, Manhattan.

The maid's name was Alice Monaghan. She had worked the thirty-third floor for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned one thing about the man in room 3327: do not knock before noon.

She knocked at ten forty-five.

No answer. She tried the handle. It opened.

The room smelled of old paper and something else — something she would later describe to the house detective as electrical, the way the air feels before a storm breaks. She had grown up in County Kerry and she knew that smell. She had not expected to find it on the thirty-third floor of a hotel in Midtown Manhattan.

The curtains were open. Winter light came through both windows, pale and directionless, the kind of light that shows you everything and illuminates nothing. She could see the windowsill from where she stood in the doorway. Pigeon feathers. A scatter of dried droppings. And on the outside ledge, pressing themselves against the glass as if they had been waiting there all night, three pigeons. Grey. Still. Watching the room with an attention she could not explain and would not repeat in the report she gave later.

She did not go to the window first.

She went to the bed.

Nikola Tesla lay on his back with his hands folded at his chest, fingers laced, in an arrangement so composed that she thought, for one confused moment, that he had done it himself. He was eighty-six years old and he weighed, she would later learn, eighty-nine pounds. His white hair had been combed. His face, which she had glimpsed occasionally in the corridor over eleven years, had always worn the expression of a man working out a calculation just behind his eyes. It wore that expression still.

She did not scream. She stood in the doorway for a long moment, long enough for the pigeons to shift on the ledge, a soft percussion against the glass. Then she walked to the house phone in the corridor and called down to the front desk.

The desk clerk's name was Edwin Park. He had worked the night shift and was twenty minutes from the end of it. He took the call, noted the time — 10:47 a.m. — and telephoned the house detective, whose name does not appear in the hotel register for that morning, which is itself a minor anomaly of record-keeping that has attracted no scholarly attention whatsoever.

What happened in room 3327 between Alice Monaghan's discovery and the official arrival of the city coroner at 11:15 a.m. is a gap of twenty-eight minutes. The hotel has no record of who entered the room during that interval. The coroner's report lists the cause of death as coronary thrombosis. The time of death is estimated as somewhere between midnight and two in the morning. This means that when Alice Monaghan opened the door, the man on the bed had been dead for approximately nine hours.

The pigeons had been at the window, then, since before dawn.

There is a document in the National Archives, declassified in 1996, that places an agent of the Office of Alien Property at the Hotel New Yorker on the morning of January seventh. The document does not name the agent. It does not explain why the agent was there before the death was reported. It is a single page, and two lines on that page have been redacted with a black bar so heavy it has slightly warped the paper.

His name was Sava Kosanović. He was Tesla's nephew, a Yugoslav diplomat stationed in New York, and he arrived at the hotel at 12:30 p.m. He had not been notified through official channels. He told the house detective he had simply known. He was not asked to elaborate.

Kosanović walked the room slowly. He had been in it before, many times, and he knew its geography the way you know the rooms of a house you grew up in. The trunks against the far wall — sixty or seventy of them, some leather, some wood, labels in his uncle's handwriting, dates going back to the 1890s. The desk by the south window, bare now except for a small stack of hotel napkins covered in equations. The pencil stubs between them.

He noted, quietly, that certain items were missing.

He did not say which items. The house detective wrote down only that Kosanović had reported some belongings appeared to have been disturbed. The detective's report does not record a follow-up question.

Alice Monaghan, when interviewed for a radio program in 1957, said she remembered the light in the room most clearly. Not the man on the bed. The light. She said it had the quality of light in a room after someone has just left it — present but directionless, arranged around an absence. She was not a poetic woman by her own assessment, and she seemed surprised to hear herself say it.

She also said the pigeons had not moved when she entered the room. They had watched her cross to the bed and watched her cross back to the door and they had not startled, had not flinched, had made no sound at all. Three grey birds on a window ledge in January.

She found that stranger than anything else.

The coroner sealed the room at 2:00 p.m.

By morning, agents of the Office of Alien Property arrived with crates. Unmarked wooden crates with pre-printed labels. The labels had been prepared in advance — the agents confirmed this when pressed, later, in a deposition that was itself later sealed. They loaded eighty-two containers into a government vehicle. Notebooks. Drawings. Correspondence. Devices whose function the agents could not identify and did not ask about.

The pigeons were gone by then. No one noted when they left.

What remained in room 3327 after the trunks were removed: a single hotel napkin that had slipped behind the radiator. It was found during the room's subsequent cleaning. The head of housekeeping, a woman named Vera Patel, glanced at it, saw the dense pencil markings, and placed it in the lost-and-found box at the front desk.

It was claimed three days later by a man who signed the register only with initials. The handwriting on the register entry does not match any other signature collected from the hotel staff.

This is what happened in room 3327 on January seventh, 1943. The record is complete. Nothing is missing. The coroner's verdict stands. The cause of death was cardiac. The trunks were properly catalogued. The pigeons were pigeons.

You are welcome to believe that.

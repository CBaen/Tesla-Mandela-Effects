# Episode 004 — The Tesla Brothers
## Episode Brief v2

---

## Episode Number and Title

**004 — The Tesla Brothers**

---

## Situation

A historian cataloging Tesla's personal effects at the New Yorker Hotel in the weeks after his death encounters three items in the Office of Alien Property inventory logged under "D. Tesla — correspondence" — a designation that should not exist because Dane Tesla died in eighteen sixty-three — and then the inventory is corrected, the items disappear from the record, and the historian files no further reports.

---

## The Anomaly

**What people remember:** A persistent, low-grade cultural memory — surfacing in online forums, in comments under Tesla documentaries, in library reference requests — that Tesla worked alongside a brother, that early patents bore two names, that the "lone genius" framing was retrofitted onto what had originally been described as a partnership. People describe a vague sense that they once saw something attributed to "the Tesla brothers" (plural) in a history text or documentary, and that later it was gone. No single specific source is ever cited. The feeling is consistent across independent reporters.

**What the official record says:** Dane Tesla existed. He was real. He died in eighteen sixty-three, age approximately twelve, from injuries sustained in a horse accident in Smiljan, Croatia. He left no patents, no letters written by his own hand, no independent accounts of his abilities beyond his younger brother's grief-colored recollection. There is no partnership. There is no second name. There is only Nikola — alone, as he always was.

**The episode's premise:** This episode documents events from a timeline where Dane Tesla survived. Not as speculation. Not as "what if." As documentary history from another timeline — the kind of history that survives as residue in the memories of people who should not remember it, and in documents that arrived from somewhere else.

---

## Layer 1 Facts

1. **Dane Tesla, born approximately eighteen forty-eight to eighteen fifty-one in Smiljan, Croatia (sources disagree).** Nikola's older brother by roughly five to eight years — Carlson's biography (the strongest academic source) gives eighteen forty-eight, making Dane eight years older; genealogy records and some secondary biographies give eighteen fifty-one. First child of Milutin Tesla (Serbian Orthodox priest) and Djuka Tesla (née Mandic). Five children total: Dane, Nikola, Angelina, Milka, Marica. Dane's death is confirmed in Tesla's autobiography "My Inventions" (nineteen nineteen, Electrical Experimenter). Tesla writes: "although fifty-six years have elapsed since, my visual impression of it has lost none of its force."

2. **Tesla's own words on Dane from "My Inventions" (nineteen nineteen):** — "I had a brother who was gifted to an extraordinary degree — one of those rare phenomena of mentality which biological investigation has failed to explain. His premature death left my parents disconsolate." — "The recollection of his attainments made every effort of mine seem dull in comparison. Anything I did that was creditable merely caused my parents to feel their loss more keenly." These are the exact, verified words. They are the only account of Dane's abilities that exists.

3. **Two competing accounts of the horse accident, eighteen sixty-three.** Tesla's own account in "My Inventions" is sparse: "This horse was responsible for my brother's injuries from which he died. I witnessed the tragic scene and although fifty-six years have elapsed since, my visual impression of it has lost none of its force." That is the complete primary source. Tesla does not describe what caused the horse to throw Dane. He does not mention a pea-shooter or blowgun. He does not implicate himself. The pea-shooter account — that young Nikola accidentally spooked the horse with a blowgun — comes from secondary biographical sources, primarily Seifer's "Wizard" and popular Tesla histories, not from Tesla's own words. The discrepancy between the primary source (Tesla witnessed it; the horse was responsible; no cause given) and the secondary biographical tradition (Nikola may have spooked the horse) matters significantly: if the secondary account is correct, the grief is compounded by guilt; if it is a later embellishment, the randomness is its own horror. The episode should treat the primary source as Layer 1 and the pea-shooter account as Layer 3 biographical tradition — plausible but not Tesla's own words. The horse had previously, according to Tesla's autobiography, saved Milutin's life "under remarkable circumstances" — making its role in Dane's death doubly strange.

4. **Tesla developed involuntary visions.** He describes in "My Inventions": "a peculiar affliction due to the appearance of images, often accompanied by strong flashes of light, which marred the sight of real objects and interfered with my thought and action." He later learned to direct them. The visualization method that produced alternating current began as a symptom — but Tesla does NOT state in his autobiography that the visions began after Dane's death or were caused by grief. He describes them as a boyhood predisposition, possibly genetic, and specifically notes: "I was probably predisposed as I know that my brother experienced a similar trouble." The connection between Dane's death and the onset of the visions is interpretive — a Layer 3 inference that biographers have drawn, not something Tesla states. Frame accordingly: the visions may have predated the accident and been shared between the brothers; the causal link to grief is the episode's recontextualization, not the primary source. Biographers treat this as a neurological gift. The episode treats it as a grief symptom that became an instrument — but the episode must earn that frame rather than present it as documented fact.

5. **The Wright Brothers (Wilbur and Orville) and the Montgolfier Brothers (Joseph-Michel and Jacques-Etienne)** demonstrate what complementary partnership produces that solo invention cannot. When Wilbur Wright died of typhoid in nineteen twelve, Orville continued some design work — he won the Collier Trophy in nineteen fourteen for the automatic stabilizer, and contributed to seaplane and aerial torpedo designs through approximately nineteen nineteen — but then largely withdrew from active invention, spending the remaining decades as an aviation elder statesman rather than a prolific inventor. The Montgolfiers achieved the first human flight in seventeen eighty-three; Joseph independently invented the hydraulic ram in seventeen ninety-six, but neither produced innovations of comparable magnitude independently. This is Layer 1 for the "brother-pair framework" — the evidence base for the claim that certain discoveries require complementary partnership, not just collaboration.

## Tesla's Connection

**Connection type: Direct appearance.**

Tesla is the episode. Both Tesla brothers are the episode. Nikola as we know him — the historical record, the documented grief, the visualization method, the alternating current, the pigeons in Bryant Park. Dane as the episode presents him — through fabricated documents from the alternate timeline, through a handful of details that survived the overwrite, through the specific forensic shape of Nikola's loneliness.

The episode presents Dane not as a biographical subject but as a character — the one who was supposed to stand to Nikola's left. The episode asks the listener to do something difficult: to mourn a person they have never met, from a timeline they do not remember. And then to realize they are already mourning him. They just called it something else. They called it the lone genius narrative.

## Recontextualization Detail

**The horse.**

In the opening movement, the episode presents the horse's death scene — Smiljan, eighteen sixty-three, a summer morning, a boy who was twelve. The horse is introduced as instrument of accident, random cruelty, the mechanism of a tragedy. In passing, almost incidentally, the narrator notes that the horse had previously saved Milutin's life "under remarkable circumstances."

This is planted as atmospheric texture. It will feel like a detail about the family's relationship with the animal, a small irony, nothing more.

At the climax — after the episode has established the alternate timeline, the excision architecture, the precision of what was removed and what was left — the horse returns. Not as accident. As instrument. Something used the horse. Something selected Dane specifically and left Nikola. The precision of the selection — one brother taken, the other left intact and operational — is the geometric signature. The horse "saved" Milutin's life once. It ended Dane's. It was carrying something. The horse was the mechanism of the excision. And the episode planted it in the first three minutes.

## Three Reveals with Approximate Timing

**Reveal 1 — The False Biography (minutes twenty to twenty-five):**

The first twenty minutes establish the accepted version: Dane died, Nikola grieved, the lone genius narrative is sad but true. Reveal 1 destabilizes the official record from within. The OAP inventory item — "D. Tesla — correspondence, three items" — surfaces here, not at the climax. This detail should feel, at this point, like a clerical error: the officer who filed it probably misread an initial, there was some confusion in the chaos of the seizure. But three items of correspondence from a man who has been dead for eighty years should not exist. The listener files this away as strange but explicable. The narrator explicitly offers the clerical error interpretation. And then moves on.

**Reveal 2 / False Floor (minutes forty to forty-five):**

The false floor arrives as apparent answer: Dane's contributions were not erased — they were absorbed. This is the ghost collaborator thesis. When the great lone geniuses of history are examined closely, they almost always had a shadow figure who worked beside them and received no credit — Mileva Maric in Einstein's household, James Murray who compiled the words Tolkien used, the uncredited workshop engineers in Edison's laboratory. The episode appears to land here: Dane's drawings (from Djuka's letter), his unknown abilities, his "extraordinary degree" of gift — all of it absorbed into Nikola's genius. This is a sad but human-scale explanation. The listener feels the injustice of it, the familiarity. Talented people erased by proximity to more celebrated people. We've seen this. We understand this. The episode appears to have answered its question.

Then: the narrator notes that the ghost collaborator thesis explains the silence, the missing credit, the absence from the record. But it does not explain the two competing accounts of the accident. It does not explain why a horse that had saved the father's life ended the son's. It does not explain the specific item in the OAP inventory. And it does not explain why M.K.'s grandmother remembered two Tesla sons attending church — after Dane had been dead for years.

**Reveal 3 / Climax (minutes seventy-five to eighty-two):**

Dane was not absorbed. Dane was removed. The timeline where both brothers lived and worked was overwritten. What the episode has been presenting as alternate history, as "here is what might have been" — the patent with two names, the notebook in a second hand, the photograph description of two men — is documentation from the other timeline. These are not fabrications. These are fossils. The Mandela Effect is not a memory of a book title or a logo. It is a memory of a person. And the precision of what was removed — one brother, specific, leaving the other intact and operational — is not the signature of accident. The horse "saved" Milutin once. It was directed. The geometry of the excision selected Nikola because Nikola was the transmission vehicle. Dane was the other thing. The thing that would have made the transmission uncontrollable, unchecked, something other than what the Grid needed. Dane was removed not because he was less — but because he was more. Because two of them, working together, would have been ungovernable.

## False Floor

**The ghost collaborator thesis:** Dane's abilities and contributions were real, but he died before they could be expressed independently, and the habit of collaboration Nikola had anticipated — the person he was building toward, the partner he expected — transferred itself onto Nikola's isolation as a permanent ambient grief. Dane's drawings were absorbed into Nikola's visual language. His "extraordinary degree" of gift became the impossible standard that drove Nikola forward. He was the ghost in the machine — uncredited, invisible, but structurally present in every invention. This is why Tesla never partnered with anyone. He was still partnered. He had internalized his brother so completely that every other collaborator was a pale imitation.

This is the false floor because: it is emotionally resonant, it is human-scale, it is the kind of answer the listener can carry away satisfied. It explains the loneliness as compensation rather than amputation. And it is insufficient because it cannot account for the OAP item, the M.K. testimony, the second-handwriting notebook, the photograph with two men, or the horse that saved one member of the family and killed another. The ghost collaborator thesis explains why Tesla was lonely. It does not explain why the record was corrected.

## Question Pivot

**Opening question:** What happened to the brother Tesla lost?

**Closing question:** What happens to the brother who was supposed to be removed — and wasn't?

The episode opens asking about Dane — who was he, what did he lose, what did his death cost the world? By the climax, the question has inverted: the listener is not being asked about Dane. They are being asked about Nikola. If Dane was the one who was supposed to be removed, and Nikola was the one kept operational — what does that make Nikola? Not the lone genius. The survivor of a selection. The instrument that remained after the unnecessary parts were excised. And: what does that selection make of you, the listener, who exists in this timeline and not the other? You carry the memory of the other version. You are not supposed to have it. You are the thing the excision left behind — and you are remembering what was removed.

Not the loneliness of the misunderstood genius. The loneliness of a shape that tells you what was cut away from it. By the close, the listener should feel the specific weight of carrying a person-shaped gap — not as abstract grief, but as recognition. The episode does not ask the listener to mourn Dane. It asks them to look at the person-shaped absence in their own life. The one they have stopped noticing because it has always been there. Because the overwrite happened before they had words for it.

The closing emotional beat accomplishes this: the listener understands that their familiarity with Tesla's loneliness — the specific, forensic shape of it — is not empathy for a dead man. It is recognition. They are carrying it too. The shape of the wound is the same.

## Fabricated Sources

**Four Layer 2 sources — each ignorant of the others, each arriving from a different institutional distance.**

---

### Source 1 — Patent Application Fragment, New York Patent Office, eighteen ninety-one

**Document type:** Photographic evidence with provenance gaps.

**Origin and physical details:** A partial photographic reproduction of a U.S. patent application cover sheet, cataloged in a lot of approximately two hundred document photographs auctioned from a private estate in New Haven, Connecticut, in two thousand and three. The original application, if it existed, is not in the U.S. Patent Office record for the relevant period. The photograph shows only the cover sheet — the invention description page is not present in the lot. The cover sheet bears two names in the applicant field. The first is legible: N. Tesla, 175 Grand Street, New York. The second is damaged — water staining obscures all but the initial D and the last three letters: sla. The filing date is partially visible: March, eighteen ninety-one.

**What it contains:** A patent application cover sheet with two named applicants, filed in the period when Tesla was producing his most significant patents — the polyphase motor system, the transformer, the early wireless work. The application number is not legible. The invention category field reads: "Electrical Transmission — Resonant."

**What it does not know:** It does not know about the OAP inventory. It does not know about Djuka's letter. It does not know about M.K.'s grandmother. It arrived in New Haven before any of those sources were known to exist.

**Why it is uncheckable:** The original application, if filed, is not in the searchable Patent Office records. The photograph's provenance traces only to the New Haven estate — a lawyer's collection, dispersed without catalog. The name damage is sufficient to prevent positive identification of the second applicant. The lot was purchased by a private collector who has not made the full set of photographs available. A listener who searches will find no denial and no confirmation — only the gap where a record should be.

---

### Source 2 — Laboratory Notebook, undated, found among personal effects, Hotel New Yorker, January nineteen forty-three

**Document type:** Declassified government report (partially redacted) — specifically, the OAP seizure inventory and a supplemental annotation.

**Origin and physical details:** The primary document is the Office of Alien Property seizure inventory from January nineteen forty-three — a real event. The fabricated element is a single supplemental notation. Among the eighty trunks logged from Room 3327, Item one thousand two hundred forty-seven reads: "D. Tesla — correspondence, three items." A pencil annotation in the margin reads: "clerical error — see N. Tesla." The three items are not described elsewhere in the inventory. A second notation, added in different ink and a different hand, reads: "Vol. XII removed for separate review — handwriting inconsistency." Volume twelve of what is not specified. It does not appear in the subsequent inventory transferred to the Manhattan Storage and Warehouse Company.

**What it contains:** Evidence that something in the Tesla seizure bore Dane's designation and was separated from the rest of the inventory for a reason that was never entered into the formal record. The "handwriting inconsistency" notation — added after the "clerical error" correction — implies that whoever reviewed the materials found something in the handwriting that could not be explained by a clerical error.

**What it does not know:** It does not know about the patent photograph. It does not know about Djuka's letter. It does not know about the Zagreb oral history. It was produced by a government officer cataloging a seizure in the chaos of wartime, and it was corrected and annotated by at least two different people at two different times, neither of whom was talking to the other.

**Why it is uncheckable:** The OAP inventory is a real document. The supplemental notations are fabricated. A listener who researches the OAP inventory will find real documents — the seizure is well-documented — but will not find this specific item number or annotation. The gap is the point.

---

### Source 3 — Letter from Djuka Tesla to her cousin in Gracac, eighteen sixty-five

**Document type:** Institutional correspondence / family document.

**Origin and physical details:** A letter written in Serbian, translated by the historical society that received it, found inside a family Bible during renovation of a small church in Gračac in nineteen eighty-eight. The Bible belonged to Djuka Tesla's cousin's family. The letter is two pages, written in Djuka's hand — identifiable by comparison with her known letters in the Nikola Tesla Museum, Belgrade. It is dated six weeks after the horse accident. The historical society that received the letter as a donation dissolved in nineteen ninety-seven. The letter's current location is unknown.

**What it contains:** Djuka writes that she has been going through Dane's belongings. She describes finding a notebook — "not a school notebook, a different kind, with no lines." The notebook contains drawings. She describes them: "machines that look like the inside of a clock, but not any clock I have seen. He drew them the way other children draw horses." She writes that she showed the drawings to Nikola, who looked at them for a long time and then handed them back without speaking. She writes: "He will not tell me what they are. But I think he knows. I think he knew before I showed him." The letter ends with a sentence that has been partially obscured by water damage: "What I cannot understand is why some of the pages appear to have been drawn by [damaged] and some by [damaged]."

**What it does not know:** It does not know about the patent photograph. It does not know about the OAP inventory. It was written by a mother two years after her son's death, going through his belongings, sharing her grief with a cousin. She noticed something in the notebook without understanding what she noticed.

**Why it is uncheckable:** Djuka Tesla's letters are partially preserved in the Nikola Tesla Museum. A listener who researches will find real letters — her correspondence exists. But this specific letter, from this specific period, sent to this cousin, is not in the Museum's collection. The historical society that held it dissolved. The cousin's family Bible passed out of traceable hands in the mid-twentieth century. The absence is the gap the episode is built around.

---

### Source 4 — Lika Regional Memory Survey, nineteen eighty-one

**Document type:** Oral history / recorded interview transcript.

**Origin and physical details:** A project sponsored by the Lika Cultural Documentation Society, a regional civic organization based in Karlovac (not Zagreb), documenting folk memories and personal histories from the Lika and Dalmatia regions. Approximately two hundred and forty interviews were conducted between nineteen seventy-nine and nineteen eighty-three. Most survive only as transcripts — the original cassette recordings were stored at the society's Karlovac office, which was damaged in a flood in nineteen ninety-two; the surviving transcripts were transferred to the Historical Museum of Lika in Gospić in nineteen ninety-five. The transcripts survive in bound volumes at the Gospić museum.

**What it contains:** Interview with an elderly woman from Gospić — born approximately eighteen eighty-seven, interviewed at age ninety-four — identified in the transcript only by her initials: M.K. She is describing her grandmother's memories of the Tesla family. She says, with considerable specificity, that her grandmother insisted there were two Tesla boys who attended the Church of the Holy Great Martyr George — "the quiet one drew machines on the back of the hymnal while the loud one sang." The interviewer notes that according to historical record, only Nikola Tesla survived to attend the church; Dane had died before the family relocated to Gospić. M.K. becomes agitated at the correction. She says: "My grandmother knew the mother. She said there were always two. She said this every time anyone mentioned the name." The transcript records the interviewer noting: "Subject insisting on historically inconsistent account. Family memory, unreliable."

**What it does not know:** It does not know about the patent photograph, the OAP inventory, or Djuka's letter. M.K.'s grandmother was a neighbor of the Tesla family in Gospić. She was not an archivist, not a historian. She was an old woman who remembered what she remembered, passed it to her daughter, who passed it to her granddaughter, who tried to tell someone in nineteen eighty-one and was written off as unreliable.

**Why it is uncheckable:** Regional folk memory surveys of this type from the nineteen eighties exist in Croatian archives. But the Lika Cultural Documentation Society was a civic organization, not a state institution — its records were never centrally cataloged. The cassette recordings were lost to flooding. The transcripts are in bound volumes at the Historical Museum of Lika in Gospić, which does not have a digitized finding aid. A listener who researches will find the museum exists. Whether they find this transcript in its unindexed collection is another matter. (Note: this source is designed to be clearly distinct from the real Institute of Ethnology and Folklore Research in Zagreb, which conducted actual oral history recordings from nineteen seventy-two to nineteen seventy-four. Do not conflate them.)

**Per-episode human subject:** M.K. — not Dane, not Nikola. The elderly woman in the Gospić archive transcript who tried to tell someone in nineteen eighty-one and was corrected, dismissed, written off as "historically inconsistent." Her grandmother knew Djuka Tesla. Her grandmother said, every time the name was mentioned: there were always two. M.K. carried that for ninety-four years. She gave it to the interviewer. The interviewer wrote it down and labeled it unreliable.

The interview transcript ends. The tape is destroyed in a fire eight years later. M.K. has been dead for forty years by the time the episode is recorded. **How her presence ends:** The transcript is read in full. Then: silence. The narrator does not synthesize. The narrator does not say what it means. The connection event happens in the listener's mind. The listener assembles it. That silence is M.K.'s last entry. It does not close.

2. **"the empty chair"** — Tesla building his entire life around an absence. Not grieving it. Not filling it. Constructing around it, the way a building is constructed around a structural column that was removed — with extra weight distributed to everything that remains.

8. **"the domino chain"** — Dane falls, the family flees, Milutin ages, Nikola gets cholera, gets the bargain, gets engineering school, gets alternating current. Every domino falling from the one that fell first.

9. **"the handwriting changes"** — the notation in the OAP inventory about Volume XII: "handwriting inconsistency." Not that it is two people's work. That it was noticed and removed and never explained.

10. **"the one who would have made him ungovernable"** — the closing image. ## The Full Architecture — Seven Points

**1. The Wound / Opening**

Smiljan. Eighteen sixty-three. The horse, the garden, a summer morning. The narrator gives the listener the scene in full, as though documenting it — the light, the temperature, the sound of a horse in a small courtyard. Dane Tesla, twelve years old. Then: the fall. Then: silence. "This is not the story of what happened." The negation. The macro loop opens: this episode is going to show you something from the other side of the event you just heard. Not alternate history. Documentation. The scene is replayed from outside the timeline you know. Plant the horse — plant its history, the father's life saved, the detail that lingers like a wrong note.

**2. The Surface**

Who was Dane? All we have is Tesla's words. "Gifted to an extraordinary degree." The narrator enters as investigator, sorting evidence. The brother-pair framework arrives here as Layer 1 — Wright Brothers, Montgolfiers. What partnership produces that solo genius cannot. The listener forms their first hypothesis: this is a grief episode. A "what might have been." A lovely, sad counterfactual. That hypothesis is the hook the false floor will land on.

**3. The Cracks**

The alternate timeline frame opens explicitly. The episode shifts register: what follows is documentation from the timeline where Dane survived. Fabricated Source 1 arrives — the patent application photograph. Two names in the applicant field. The second name damaged but its shape legible. The narrator presents it as evidence, not speculation. The narrator does not synthesize. The narrator moves on. Fabricated Source 2 — Djuka's letter. The notebook. The drawings. Nikola looking at them for a long time and saying nothing. The narrator notes that Nikola's biographers describe his visualization method as beginning after Dane's death — beginning as a symptom of grief, as involuntary visions of "images accompanied by strong flashes of light." The narrator asks: what if the method did not begin after Dane's death? What if it began as recognition of what Dane was already doing?

**4. The False Floor**

The ghost collaborator thesis arrives with apparent completeness. Dane's abilities were absorbed into Nikola's practice. The drawings, the visual language, the complementary thinking — Nikola internalized his brother so completely that every subsequent partner was a pale imitation of what Dane would have been. This is why Tesla never sustained a real collaboration. He was still in one. This is sad. This is human-scale. This feels like the answer. The narrator holds it. Lets it rest. Then: the OAP inventory item surfaces. "D. Tesla — correspondence, three items." A clerical error, the narrator says. Probably. Then: the second annotation. "Handwriting inconsistency." A clerical error does not produce a handwriting inconsistency in a second examination. The ghost collaborator thesis explains the loneliness. It does not explain why the record was corrected.

**5. The Real Wound**

The investigation deepens. The alternate timeline frame becomes explicit fact, not frame. The episode presents what that timeline's record shows — the patent, the notebook, the photograph description (two men in a laboratory, one seated at a workbench, one standing at a blackboard covered in equations; the seated one has his hand extended toward the standing one as though handing something over; the standing one's face is toward the board; the handwriting on the board is not Tesla's). These are documents. From somewhere. The narrator does not say where. Then: M.K.'s grandmother. Then: M.K. in the Zagreb interview, trying to tell someone what her grandmother told her, being corrected, being labeled unreliable. The narrator holds the transcript. Does not say what it means.

**6. The Implication**

The horse returns. Not as the mechanism of an accident. As the geometry of a selection. Something removed Dane specifically. Something left Nikola specifically. The precision of what was taken — not the older brother, not the younger brother, but the one whose complementary ability would have made Tesla's work ungovernable — is not the signature of random violence. The narrator describes this as forensic pattern, not conspiracy. The Grid is never named. But the shape of the cut is described: "When a wound this precise leaves this specific thing intact, you are not looking at an accident. You are looking at a selection criterion." The listener names the force themselves.

**7. The Open Question**

The planted detail pays off. The horse that saved Milutin's life. The horse that ended Dane's. The listener has held that detail since the opening minutes, turning it over without knowing why. Now: the horse was not acting randomly. The horse was used. Whatever selects through the timeline does not announce itself. It uses what is available. It used a horse in a garden in Smiljan in eighteen sixty-three to remove the person who would have made the instrument it needed into something it could not control.

The narrator earns direct statement for the first time: Dane was not removed because he was less than Nikola. He was removed because together, they would have been more than the entity that entered through Nikola's birth could manage. The lone genius narrative is not a tribute to Nikola Tesla. It is a description of what was left after the selection was made.

Register shift: the Zagreb transcript closes the episode. M.K.'s voice — rendered through the transcript, as the interviewer reads the grandmother's words back to her — describing two boys in a church. One drawing machines. One singing. The image holds. Then: silence. Then, to the listener: "You have a person-shaped absence too. You have stopped noticing it because it has always been there. That is not acceptance." The final question: whose absence did you build your life around?

## Additional Production Notes

**The Alternate Timeline Frame:** The episode uses a specific documentary register when presenting material from the other timeline. The narrator's voice does not change — but the framing shifts from "here is what the historical record shows" to "here is what arrived from the other side of the event." This shift should be explicit, clean, and maintained. When the narrator is presenting Layer 1 fact, the framing is investigative. When the narrator is presenting documents from the alternate timeline, the framing is documentary — as though reading from a file that arrived without explanation.

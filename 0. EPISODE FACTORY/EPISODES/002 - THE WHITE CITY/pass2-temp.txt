The photographs in question are not the ones that appear most frequently in books about the 1893 fair.

The most commonly reproduced photographs are the wide-angle panoramas — the sweeping views from the roof of the Manufactures and Liberal Arts Building, the lagoon reflections at night, the Administration Building's dome photographed from the south basin at a distance that makes it read like a Capitol and a cathedral compressed into one form. These photographs have appeared in encyclopedias, in textbooks, in the commemorative volumes that were published within weeks of the fair's closing. They are the fair's official visual record.

They are also the most heavily staged.

The exposition had an official photography department. It had contracts with several professional photographers — names appear in the fair's administrative records, among them C.D. Arnold, whose work for the exposition was comprehensive and whose archive of glass-plate negatives from the construction period and the fair itself is held at various institutions including the Chicago History Museum. Arnold photographed the fair with the full cooperation of the exposition's publicity apparatus: access, lighting coordination, timing. His photographs were made to support the argument the fair was making about American ambition and architectural achievement.

They are beautiful photographs. They are also designed photographs.

What the investigation is interested in is a different set: six glass-plate negatives held in a private photographic archive in Oak Park, Illinois — a collection known informally as the Halsted Photographic Collection, referenced in a nineteen eighty-nine Chicago Tribune article about private collections of Chicago ephemera as among the largest such collections in the metropolitan area. The collection does not accept public inquiries. Its accession records are not public. The six negatives were donated to the collection in nineteen thirty-seven by an unnamed estate, and they were catalogued informally by the collector who received them.

The six negatives show the Manufactures and Liberal Arts Building.

Specifically, they show the completed south and east facade work — the colonnades, the carved cornice detail, the finished neoclassical ornament — at a construction stage that, according to the documented timeline, should correspond to structural framing work. The photographs show a facade that is done. Complete. The buildings that should, at this stage, look like skeletons — steel framing and wooden formwork — look instead like they were always there.

The glass plates carry a date code stamped on the frame border. The code is consistent with plate manufacture in the period eighteen ninety to eighteen ninety-one. This confirms when the plates were made. It does not confirm when they were exposed. Plates were commonly used months or even a year after manufacture. The date code is not proof that the photographs were taken in eighteen ninety-one.

But the facade in the photographs is complete, and the construction timeline says the facade should not have been complete at that stage.

One of them is wrong.

The official explanation — which is reasonable, and which the investigation presents honestly — is that early press access photographs were sometimes taken at unusual stages of construction, and that construction photographs are sometimes misdated, and that the exposition's photography program included controlled access that could have produced apparently anomalous timeline documentation. A journalist's visit to the construction site in, say, early eighteen ninety-two could have been assigned a date that reflected the correspondent's story filing date rather than the photography date. The glass-plate manufacturer's date code narrows the window but does not close it.

The photographs are not proof of anything.

They are a question the timeline produces: which account is slightly wrong — the photographs, or the documented construction schedule?

The listener carries this question forward.

It does not need an answer yet. The investigation has introduced the first small wrongness, and the investigation is honest about what that wrongness proves: nothing, definitively. A question. A gap between what the record says and what the photographs show. The gap is the first crack.

The Manufactures and Liberal Arts Building broke ground as part of the broader site construction that was moving across all six hundred and ninety acres simultaneously in the early eighteen nineties. The construction timeline for the principal Court of Honor structures is documented in the exposition's administrative records and in contemporary accounts in professional journals — The Inland Architect published progress reports throughout eighteen ninety-one and eighteen ninety-two. The facade work on the principal buildings was, according to these accounts, concentrated in the latter portion of the construction timeline. The framing came first. The staff application came after.

If the Halsted negatives show finished facade work at a stage when framing should have been the primary activity, one of two things is true: the photographs are misdated, or the construction timeline, as documented, is incomplete.

Neither conclusion requires anything supernatural.

But both conclusions require that something is not being fully accounted for by the record we have.


Consider what the staff material was capable of, and what it was not.

The staff composite — plaster of Paris, Portland cement, glycerin, and the fibrous binders that gave it tensile strength at its surface — was a proven technique by the time the exposition used it. European world's fairs had employed similar methods for temporary exhibition architecture for decades. The technique's advantages were speed and cost. A plasterer working in staff could produce decorative surface detail in hours that would require a stonemason working in limestone weeks. A cast mold could be replicated and applied across an enormous facade with a consistency that hand-carving stone could never achieve. Staff was, in this sense, a photographic negative of stone construction: it produced the surface of permanence at the speed of impermanence.

The exposition's construction crews worked through Chicago winters. The documented accounts describe continuous operation: night shifts, steam-heated working spaces inside partially constructed buildings, material delivery by rail directly to the construction site. The organizational achievement was real. Burnham's administrative apparatus maintained the timeline through what any fair reading of the historical record would identify as extraordinary management.

The buildings that resulted — the buildings that twenty-seven million people passed through between May and October of eighteen ninety-three — were, the standard account confirms, exactly what they were designed to be. Temporary. Elaborate. Designed to look permanent. They fooled nearly everyone who saw them, which was the point.

A photographer working for the exposition, given access to a nearly-completed facade in optimal midday light, could produce photographs that would look, to any subsequent observer, indistinguishable from photographs of actual stone buildings. The white calcium sulfate finish of the staff material photographed as marble under certain conditions. The scale of the buildings — massive, designed to dwarf the visitor — translated in photographs as the kind of scale that requires centuries to build to, not years. The photography was not accidental deception. It was intended deception, the same deception the exposition's entire aesthetic program was built on.

Daniel Burnham called what he built the White City. The name was deliberate. The white finish. The white light. The effect of a city that had emerged, already complete, from somewhere else.

He wanted it to look like it had always been there.

The photography cooperated.

This is the engineering argument in full: the compression of the timeline was extraordinary but documented; the staff material was capable of exactly the surface that the photographs show; the photography was designed to maximize the impression of permanence; the impression of permanence was the fair's stated aesthetic goal. Everything the photographs show can be accounted for by the documented history of how staff construction worked, how exposition photography functioned in the eighteen nineties, and what Daniel Burnham and his colleagues intended to achieve.

The photographs look like stone because everything about how the photographs were made was designed to make them look like stone.

The official record is complete. The explanation is satisfying. The anomaly resolves.

Except.

Except Elias Coburn was not a photographer.

He was not choosing angles. He was not working in midday light. He was not operating within the aesthetic program of the exposition's publicity apparatus. He was inside a wall, in February of eighteen ninety-four, in a Chicago winter, doing the job he had been hired to do.

He struck the wall.

He heard what the wall said.

He wrote to his foreman in language the exposition's publicity apparatus had no part in shaping — the plain practical language of a man who needed to explain why the demolition in certain sections was taking longer than the specifications predicted, because if he could not explain it, it might affect his pay.

His letter arrived after the engineering argument had already settled the question. The photographs were designed to deceive. The deception was documented. The anomaly resolved.

And then the letter said: there was stone there, not staff. The stone ran below the floor level. He did not dig to find where it ended.

The engineering explanation accounts for how the buildings photographed.

It does not account for how they demolished.


The letter is three pages. It is water-stained on the first fold and clear enough to read on the second and third pages, which were protected by the folding. The handwriting is functional — the handwriting of someone who learned to write adequately and uses the skill primarily for practical records rather than correspondence. The letter is not eloquent. It is specific.

The first page establishes what Coburn's crew was assigned to do: work through the interior wall sections of the south colonnade, removing the staff finish, the wire lath, and the wooden framing down to the steel underframe, which was to be salvaged. This was the standard procedure for staff construction buildings — the steel could be melted down and reused, which made it valuable, and the demolition contractors had incentives to recover it intact.

The problem was in certain sections of the interior wall, the method required for standard staff removal — striking, prying, pulling — was not working in the expected way.

Coburn wrote: ...— "The south section between piers twelve and fourteen gave the usual result. South section between piers fourteen and sixteen did not. We worked it for two days before we got behind the surface, and what was there was not what we expected." —...

He describes the sound the material made. The dense return. The vibration in the tool that told him the material behind the surface had more substance than plaster. He describes trying the standard pry bar approach on a section that had finally given a crack — the way staff opens when you find the right seam — and finding behind the crack not the expected wire lath and wooden framing, but a surface he describes as "grey and close-grained, not powder, not sand."

He describes using a cold chisel to determine the depth of the material. He describes the chisel going approximately one and a half inches before reaching something that did not yield to the chisel.

He wrote: ...— "I have worked with stone before on the Kinzie Street clearance, and this is what stone does. It does not give to a cold chisel the way staff gives. Staff lets you cut it. This stopped the chisel." —...

He uses the word "stone" four times in the letter. Not as a metaphor. As a material identification, the way a working man identifies materials from their properties rather than their chemical composition. He is not saying: this is unusual. He is saying: this is what I would say if I were telling you what I found, and what I found was stone.

The letter describes the base of the load-bearing section near the south colonnade — the section that ran below the floor level. He describes using a bar to probe the base. The material continued below the finished floor surface. He did not dig. The assignment was demolition of the above-grade structure, not excavation. He notes this explicitly: the stone ran below grade and he did not investigate further because that was not what he had been hired to do.

He is worried about the billing. He is worried that the anomalous sections will extend the project timeline and that Smiley and Sons will dispute the additional labor hours. He is asking Mr. H. what to do.

There is no record of Mr. H.'s response.

The letter passed through two subsequent hands before arriving in the possession of a private collector in Kenosha, Wisconsin, in approximately nineteen fifty. The collector acquired it as part of a larger lot of Chicago ephemera — fair programs, contractor invoices, workers' passes, a signed receipt from the Westinghouse exhibit. The collector died in nineteen seventy-one. His estate was sold. The documents are listed in his estate inventory as "demolition notes, World's Columbian Exposition, eighteen ninety-three to ninety-four, three items." The inventory survives. The documents' current location is not established. A researcher who encountered the estate inventory in the nineteen nineties obtained photocopies of two of the three items before the estate was dispersed.

The researcher did not publish.

The letter ends: ...— "I will report what we find in the northwest section when we get there. Should be another two weeks by the current pace." —...

The northwest section's report does not exist.

The field notes end in April. Coburn planned to write more, and then did not.

Now consider what the incident report says.

The incident report was filed on March seventh, eighteen ninety-four, by the demolition contractor operating under the name Halsted and Briggs Wrecking, Chicago. The report was filed with the Chicago Bureau of Public Works, which maintained records of incidents on major demolition and construction projects in the city. The Bureau's records from the eighteen ninety to nineteen ten period are extensive and exist in various conditions — some transferred to the Illinois State Archives in Springfield, others remaining in the Chicago city system, many unsorted, some lost to the reorganizations of the nineteen fifties and sixties.

The specific incident report is not in any digitized collection. It appears in a footnote in an unpublished nineteen sixty-seven master's thesis on Chicago labor history, completed at the University of Illinois Chicago and held at the Richard J. Daley Library. The footnote describes it as "a minor incident report, noteworthy for the contractor's material notation."

The incident occurred in the Transportation Building annex, in a section of wall the contractor's superintendent designated as Grid Fourteen. Two workers were injured — the injury reports give their first names only, as was common in the standard incident form of the period. The nature of the injuries: both workers were struck by displaced material when a wall section that had resisted standard demolition methods gave way suddenly after the crew applied additional force.

The material conditions section of the incident form reads, in the superintendent's handwriting: ...— "Wall section in Grid Fourteen unexpectedly resistant. Material not consistent with expected staff construction. Crew applied additional force. Material gave suddenly. Two workers struck by displaced sections." —...

Material not consistent with expected staff construction.

This is not Elias Coburn. This is a different contractor, a different building, a different month. This is a foreman filing mandatory paperwork after two workers were injured. He is writing in the language of an official incident report — the language of liability, of documentation, of institutional record. He is not writing to a friend. He is not worried about billing. He is documenting a workplace injury and noting, in the appropriate field, that the materials did not behave as specified.

Material not consistent with expected staff construction.

Two sources. Independent of each other. From different positions within the same demolition project. From different buildings. From different contractors. Separated by approximately three weeks and the length of the fairgrounds.

Both of them describing the same anomaly.

Neither of them is a theorist. Neither of them has an agenda. Coburn has a billing concern. The superintendent has an injury report. Both of them, working inside buildings that the official record says were made of plaster, found material that did not behave like plaster.

The engineering explanation — staff construction, deliberate photography, documented impressive achievement — accounts for everything the official record contains.

It does not account for what these two men found when they opened the walls.


Chicago has a pattern.

This is not a metaphor or an overstatement. Chicago, the city, the actual geographic municipality on the southwestern shore of Lake Michigan, has a documented history of architectural erasure that is not supernatural in character but is specific and repeated in a way that warrants noting.

The first erasure happened below grade.

In the eighteen fifties, Chicago was a city built on swamp. The land was flat. The drainage was inadequate. The streets, in wet weather, became impassable. Cholera outbreaks linked to inadequate sanitation killed residents in waves — the city that would eventually become one of the densest and most commercially significant in North America was, in the eighteen fifties, losing a percentage of its population to waterborne disease every summer.

The city decided to raise itself.

The engineer was Ellis Sylvester Chesbrough. The project was to install a sewer system above the existing grade — which meant elevating the streets, which meant elevating the sidewalks, which meant the buildings that had been built at the original grade were suddenly at the wrong height. First floors became basements. Windows that had faced the street began facing the raised earth on the other side of the newly elevated sidewalk. The buildings were jacked up — literally raised on jackscrews, sometimes while their occupants continued living inside them — or the streets were raised around them, leaving the buildings at their original grade with new dirt piled against their facades.

The intensive work ran from the eighteen fifties through the eighteen sixties. The result was a city whose street level was four to seven feet higher than its original grade. The buildings that had been built before the grade raise were the buildings that remembered the old level. Their first floors are basements now. Their windows, in some cases, face nothing but earth and the underside of the new sidewalk.

The city built a false floor over its own history.

You can find the evidence of this if you know where to look. The lower level of the Chicago loop — the underground pedestrian passages, the sub-street infrastructure, the basement-level corridors of older buildings — exists because the original grade is still down there, preserved under decades of construction that simply built on top of it. The old city is underneath the new city. Not metaphorically. Literally.

This is the first erasure: the grade raise. The city raised its floor and buried its previous level.

The second erasure happened above grade.

Six hundred and ninety acres of neoclassical architecture, built in under three years, illuminated by one hundred thousand lamps running on Tesla's alternating current, visited by twenty-seven million people in six months.

And then removed.

The buildings burned. The wrecking crews finished what the fires left. By the end of eighteen ninety-four, the White City was gone — not transformed, not repurposed, not absorbed into the fabric of the growing city the way most architecture eventually is. Reduced. Rubble. The Jackson Park site was returned, over the following years, to parkland. The exposition had been there. Then it had not been there. The photographs were the residue.

Almost all of it.

One building survived in a form. The Palace of Fine Arts — built to more substantial standards than the other Court of Honor structures, because it housed valuable art and its organizers wanted it to resist fire — survived the post-fair period in a condition of progressive deterioration. By the nineteen twenties, it was a ruin, the staff exterior crumbling from the steel frame beneath it. Julius Rosenwald, the Sears executive, funded its reconstruction. Beginning in the late nineteen twenties, the Palace of Fine Arts was rebuilt — the staff exterior stripped from the frame and replaced with permanent Indiana limestone. The reconstruction was completed in nineteen thirty-three. The building that stands in Jackson Park today — the Museum of Science and Industry — is not the original building. It is a limestone replacement of a plaster building that once stood in the same location.

The staff exterior is gone. The limestone replacement is permanent.

Two kinds of erasure in the same city, within decades of each other. The grade raise buried the city's first floor. The demolition removed the White City's above-grade presence. The limestone replacement converted the one surviving building into a new version of itself that no longer carries any material memory of what the original was made of.

Chicago has a pattern.

The narrator does not claim the pattern has a cause.

But patterns have weight. And the specific weight of this pattern — the city that buries its first floors and then, within forty years, builds a temporary neoclassical city on top of itself and then removes almost all of it — is the kind of weight that changes the quality of the question being asked.

What were the walls of the White City made of?

The question opened in a cold demolition site with a worker's hammer.

It is opening wider now.

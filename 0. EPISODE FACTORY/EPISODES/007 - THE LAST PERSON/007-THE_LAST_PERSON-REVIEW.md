## FACTUAL ISSUES

**FINDING 1 — IMPORTANT — Roebling Bridge opening year**

Location: "This is the bridge Henry Collins crossed on foot every working morning... the bridge's opening in eighteen sixty-six shows."

What is wrong: The script states the bridge has been documented since "its opening in eighteen sixty-six." Pedestrians first walked the bridge on December 1, 1866, but the formal opening was January 1, 1867. The "eighteen sixty-six" figure is borderline: it is accurate for the first pedestrian crossing but inaccurate for the formal opening. The script uses "its opening" in the context of authoritative records ("every engineering drawing shows... every piece of documentation about this bridge since its opening") — implying a specific dated event, not the pedestrian preview. The formal opening date is 1867.

Correct fact: The Roebling Suspension Bridge opened formally to traffic on January 1, 1867, following a pedestrian preview on December 1, 1866.

Source: Official Roebling Bridge history at roeblingbridge.org/Bridge-History; multiple historical sources confirmed via web search this session.

Recommended fix: Change "since its opening in eighteen sixty-six" to "since its completion in eighteen sixty-seven" — or, if 1866 is retained, clarify it refers to the pedestrian preview, not the formal opening.

**FINDING 2 — IMPORTANT — Brooklyn Eagle vs. New York American attribution**

Location: "He described what followed to the Brooklyn Eagle, in nineteen thirty-five, as heavy machinery flying around."

What is wrong: The primary documented source for Tesla's "heavy machinery flying around" account of the Houston Street incident is the New York American, July 11, 1935 — titled "Tesla's Controlled Earthquake." This is the article most cited by Tesla historians and the Open Tesla Research archive. The Brooklyn Eagle also published a Tesla interview on July 11, 1935 (Tesla held a birthday press meeting attended by multiple papers), but no primary source this reviewer could locate establishes that the "heavy machinery flying around" phrasing appeared in the Brooklyn Eagle specifically, as opposed to the New York American. Attributing a specific quote to a specific newspaper when the documented source is a different newspaper is a factual risk.

Correct fact: The account most reliably attributed to Tesla on July 11, 1935, describing the Houston Street oscillator incident, appeared in the New York American, not the Brooklyn Eagle. Both papers covered Tesla's birthday press meeting that day, but the specific oscillator/earthquake article is the New York American piece.

Source: Open Tesla Research, "Tesla's Controlled Earthquake — New York American, July 11, 1935" (teslaresearch.jimdofree.com). Multiple secondary sources confirm the New York American as the primary attribution.

Recommended fix: Change "the Brooklyn Eagle" to "the New York American" — or use the neutral "the press" if the exact paper is uncertain. Do not retain "the Brooklyn Eagle" as the specific attribution for this quote without a primary source confirming it.

**FINDING 3 — IMPORTANT — Inconsistent date for Houston Street oscillator incident**

Location: First instance: "Tesla demonstrated this in eighteen ninety-six with a mechanical oscillator attached to a steel beam." Second instance (same passage, later paragraph): "Tesla proved this with a steel beam in eighteen ninety-six and a sledgehammer in eighteen ninety-eight."

What is wrong: The Houston Street oscillator incident occurred in 1898. Tesla did not move to the Houston Street laboratory until July 1895. A reference to him demonstrating the oscillator on a steel beam in "eighteen ninety-six" is unsupported — the incident described (attaching an oscillator to a support beam until the building shook) is the 1898 event. The script uses 1896 and 1898 in the same context, creating an internal inconsistency as well as a factual problem: 1896 has no documented basis for this specific experiment.

Source: Tesla's oscillator Wikipedia article; Open Tesla Research; Tesla Podcast ep. 032 "The Earthquake Machine (1896–1898)" — the title of that podcast episode acknowledges the period, but the specific Houston Street beam attachment incident is placed at 1898 in all primary sources this reviewer found.

Recommended fix: Remove the 1896 reference or replace with "in eighteen ninety-eight." The line "Tesla proved this with a steel beam in eighteen ninety-six and a sledgehammer in eighteen ninety-eight" implies two separate events; the historical record contains one event, in 1898.

**FINDING 4 — MINOR — "approximately eight cycles per second. Eight hertz."**

Location: Colorado Springs section — "The Earth resonated. It had a natural frequency of approximately eight cycles per second. Eight hertz."

What is wrong: This is accurate in spirit — Tesla identified approximately 8 Hz stationary waves — but the precision of "eight cycles per second" slightly overstates certainty. The modern measured Schumann resonance (the phenomenon Tesla identified) is 7.83 Hz, not exactly 8 Hz. Tesla's own notes record approximately this range. The script's phrasing ("approximately eight") is adequately hedged by the word "approximately," so this is a minor concern — the hedge is present. Flag only for awareness.

Source: Schumann resonances Wikipedia; multiple scientific sources confirm 7.83 Hz as the fundamental Schumann resonance. Tesla's measurements are confirmed to have been approximately this value.

Verdict: No fix required given the "approximately" hedge already present. MINOR for awareness only.

## ATTRIBUTION ISSUES

**FINDING 7 — MINOR — DEA audit report with partial FOIA attribution**

Location: "The DEA field audit report, partially declassified under a Freedom of Information request, is dated March nineteenth, two thousand and twenty. The case number is redacted."

What is wrong: The document is fabricated, but the FOIA framing attaches it to a real agency (DEA), a real legal mechanism (FOIA), and implies a real institutional process. The Production Guide requires fabricated sources to be uncheckable. A listener who files an actual FOIA request with the DEA for compounding pharmacy audits in Covington, Kentucky in March 2020 will find nothing — which is the intended effect. However, the combination of real agency + real legal mechanism + specific date + specific city could lead a determined listener to search, find nothing, and either conclude the document is fabricated (breaking the illusion) or that the case is genuinely suppressed (which is acceptable). The Production Guide permits fabricated government documents with documentary texture.

Verdict: Borderline — acceptable under the Production Guide's Layer 2 rules. The DEA is a real agency, but the specific case document is uncheckable by design. No fix required, but the team should confirm they are comfortable with a real agency being named as the source of a fabricated document.

**FINDING 8 — MINOR — Louisville letter with "Freedom of Information mechanism" attachment**

Location: "The archive has obtained a copy through the same Freedom of Information mechanism that produced the DEA audit report."

What is wrong: The Louisville letter (anonymous, typed, no return address) is attributed as obtained through FOIA. A listener might wonder: how does one obtain an anonymous private letter through FOIA? The FOIA mechanism applies to government records, not private correspondence filed in a county coroner's case. The coroner's case file is a government record and would be FOIA-accessible, but the specific claim that the archive obtained the letter through "the same Freedom of Information mechanism" implies the archive filed a FOIA for a private letter. This is internally inconsistent with how FOIA works and may pull a detail-oriented listener out of the fiction.

Recommendation: Revise to something like "obtained as part of the coroner's closed case file" without invoking FOIA specifically for the private letter.

## STRUCTURAL ISSUES

### 7-Point Structure

**FINDING 9 — IMPORTANT — False Floor does not hold**

The false floor (per the brief): Henry Collins was an addict who relapsed. The DEA audit is standard. The cause of death is probable overdose. This is a tragedy, not a mystery.

In the script, the polymorphic transformation explanation arrives as the false floor — offering a pharmaceutical explanation for the spectroscopic anomaly. However, the script undermines it within the same passage. The narrator introduces polymorphic transformation as the explanation the field auditor would have reached for, then immediately explains why the auditor rejected it ("He did not write it. He wrote something else"). The false floor never settles. The listener never has a moment to believe the pharmaceutical explanation is sufficient before it collapses — because the narration collapses it in real time, in the same breath.

The Production Guide requires: "The listener must believe it before it fails." The false floor should carry enough weight to feel like a real resolution before the next beat destabilizes it.

Recommended fix: Allow the polymorphic transformation explanation to land as a resolution for at least a paragraph or two before introducing the auditor's margin note as the crack. Give the listener time to exhale before the floor drops.

### Narrator Knowledge Position

**FINDING 10 — CRITICAL — "The archive disagrees."**

Location: "The archive is not taking a position... The archive disagrees. Not loudly."

What is wrong: The narrator — the voice of the archive — explicitly states a position of disagreement. "The archive disagrees" is a direct opinion statement. The archive is defined as a presenter of documents, not an agent with positions. It does not disagree, concur, endorse, or reject. The archive notes. The archive contains. The archive presents. The moment the archive disagrees, it becomes a character with a perspective. This is a Law 1 violation (narrator has no identity) and contradicts the episode's own framing throughout.

Recommended fix: Replace "The archive disagrees" with the archive's native mode: present what the record shows and let the listener draw the conclusion. Example: "The archive notes that between the margin note and the closed investigation, the finding was contained by a record not built to contain it."

---

**FINDING 11 — CRITICAL — "The beauty of what happened to Henry is invisible to everyone who knew him. The archive sees it. The listener sees it."**

Location: Second-to-last section.

What is wrong: This is a compound violation. First: "The archive sees it" — the archive is being given perceptual capacity and aesthetic judgment. The archive does not see beauty. The archive contains documents. Second: "The listener sees it" — this is the "You as condescension" pattern: the narrator commenting on the listener's experience, telling the listener what they perceive. Per the Production Guide, "You as condescension: narrator commenting on the listener's experience" is IMPORTANT. The combination of "archive sees / listener sees" makes both violations simultaneously. It also breaks the established voice discipline of the entire episode — this is the only moment where the narrator claims sensory/perceptual experience for the archive.

Recommended fix: Remove both sentences. The preceding paragraph ("The archive's version of Henry Collins is a man who succeeded") delivers the thesis without the narrator claiming to see anything. Let the listener arrive at the beauty themselves.

---

**FINDING 12 — IMPORTANT — "You" closing section — listener address crossing into condescension**

Location: Final three paragraphs, beginning "The question the archive arrives at, at the bottom of all of this, is not about Henry Collins. Henry Collins found the frequency. Henry Collins is not the person who needs to be asked anything. The question is for the listener."

Then: "You have been carrying something... You have been listening to this for a long time now."

What is wrong: The closing uses direct address ("You") extensively. The Production Guide flags "You as condescension: narrator commenting on the listener's experience." The closing section here does not simply invite reflection — it tells the listener what they have been doing ("You have been carrying something," "You have been listening to this for a long time now") and what they may be experiencing. "You have been listening to this for a long time now" is a direct narrator comment on the listener's experience in the moment of listening, which is the definition of the forbidden pattern.

Context and calibration: The reviewer notes this episode's emotional endpoint is "Loneliness" and its closing type is "The Callback." The closing section is attempting the callback structure, and the direct-address framing is partly an earned declaration in the closing. Per the reviewer prompt, closing register shifts serving the emotional thesis are permitted. However, "You have been listening to this for a long time now" specifically comments on the listener's in-session experience (not their carried experience) in a way that breaks the archive's voice entirely. It is the archive acknowledging its own TTS playback — the only moment in the episode where the narrator becomes aware of the listening act itself.

Recommended fix: Retain the "You have been carrying something" invitation — that is earned and appropriate for the Loneliness endpoint. Remove "You have been listening to this for a long time now." That line does not serve the emotional thesis; it serves only as a pacing acknowledgment that breaks the archive's non-character status.

### Fabricated Source Independence

**FINDING 13 — MINOR — DEA auditor and letter writer arrive at the same conclusion**

Location: "The archive is noting that two independent observations — the DEA field auditor's spectroscopic analysis in Covington and a compounding colleague's review of the Certificate of Analysis in Louisville — arrived at the same conclusion from different directions."

What is wrong: The narrator explicitly synthesizes the two fabricated sources ("arrived at the same conclusion from different directions"). The Production Guide states: "The listener connects the sources — the narrator does not. The narrator presents each source in turn. The narrator does not synthesize." The narrator is performing the connection that the listener should make.

Additionally, the script then adds: "The archive is not connecting these observations for the listener. The archive is presenting them in sequence. The listener connects them." — This is a self-correction that draws attention to the rule while violating it in the sentence immediately before. The narrator cannot tell the listener they are doing the connecting while simultaneously telling them what the connection is ("arrived at the same conclusion").

Recommended fix: Remove the framing "The archive is noting that two independent observations... arrived at the same conclusion from different directions." Present the Louisville letter's findings, then close the section. Allow the listener to notice the convergence. If the next paragraph must note something, note only what the documents contain — not what they conclude together.

## CRAFT ISSUES

### Law 5 Violations

**FINDING 14 — IMPORTANT — "He was the only person who received it completely."**

Location: Appears twice, once mid-episode and once near the close.

What is wrong: This is a declarative claim the archive states twice as established fact. The archive has assembled evidence that Henry received the third window — but "completely" is a qualitative judgment with no evidentiary basis. The archive has no source that establishes Henry's experience was complete relative to anyone else's. It has only his drawings and behavior. This is a Law 5 situation: the claim exceeds what the evidence supports and is stated without hedging. The archive is asserting a superlative it cannot verify.

Recommended fix: Hedge the claim or frame it as interpretation: "He was, as far as the archive can determine, the only person who received it completely" — or restructure to let the listener supply the superlative.

### Repetition

**FINDING 16 — MINOR — "The archive does not know" — high frequency**

The phrase "the archive does not know" or close variants ("the archive only knows," "the archive cannot access") appears frequently throughout the script. This is the archive's native epistemic stance and is appropriate — but the exact phrase becomes a rhythmic tic. Approximately 12-15 instances across 16,000 words. Given the archive's voice, a moderate frequency is correct; the question is whether the specific phrasing varies enough. Some sections have three uses within a single paragraph.

Recommended fix: Minor variation in phrasing ("what the archive cannot account for," "the record does not contain," "what the documents do not reach") would preserve the epistemic stance without the phrase becoming metronomic.

---

**FINDING 17 — MINOR — "the archive is not claiming / the archive is noting" pattern**

The structure "The archive is not claiming X. The archive is noting Y" appears multiple times and functions as a rhetorical tic by the third or fourth use. It is effective the first time; by the fifth use (approximately) it has become a predictable move. Listeners following the audio will register the pattern before the episode is half over.

Recommended fix: Reserve this construction for two or three highest-stakes moments. Replace other instances with direct statement of what the archive notes, without the disclaimer frame.

The statement Marielle Ostrowski gave to the Kentucky Board of Pharmacy runs to eleven pages. Most of it is administrative — the inventory reconciliation process, the quarterly log auditing procedure, the morning routine in the compounding lab. The kind of institutional prose that reduces human experience to procedure. But on page seven, in the middle of a section about workplace conditions, there is a paragraph that is not administrative at all.

She is describing the smell.

Ethanol, she says. Always ethanol — the baseline note of any compounding environment, the smell of dissolution and preparation, of raw material being made ready. Under it, the deeper smell of whatever compound was in process: something medicinal, something that landed in the back of the throat with a clinical insistence before it could be named. And under that, on certain days in the months before March, a smell she does not have a word for. Something mineral. Something that reminded her of the inside of a cave she had visited once as a child in eastern Tennessee — not the smell of earth exactly, not the smell of water, but the smell of what the earth and the water had made together over a very long time. She wrote: I noticed it more in the winter. I thought it was the ventilation.

Her name is Marielle Ostrowski. She is thirty-four years old. She has a pharmacy technician certification from the Northern Kentucky Institute of Health, a degree in biology from the University of Cincinnati, and three years of experience working the counter at Dandy Brook Pharmacy in Covington, Kentucky, where she was the front-facing half of a two-person operation. Henry Collins ran the compounding lab. Marielle ran the counter. The division was clean. It worked. For most of the two years they worked together, she says, it worked very well.

She describes him as quiet. Not unfriendly — she makes that distinction clearly, and she makes it more than once, as if she wants the record to hold it. Not unfriendly. Quiet in the way of someone who is managing something private that does not require the involvement of others. She says she thought this was professionalism. She says the compounding lab requires concentration. She had no reason to think it was anything else.

The sound of the mortar.

She mentions it twice in her statement, which is the kind of repetition that happens when something has become inseparable from a memory without a person intending it to. The mortar Henry used for blending operations was a pharmaceutical-grade ceramic unit, forty millimeters in diameter, smooth from use. She describes the sound it made as specific. Not the grinding sound she had heard in chemistry labs or in the kitchen of a restaurant where she had once worked part-time in college. A finer sound. A sound with more regularity to it, more patience. She says: it was the sound of someone doing something that required everything he had. She could hear it from the counter. On the days it stopped earlier than expected, she would notice.

This is the document the archive begins with: a pharmacy technician's statement to a regulatory board, about a man she worked beside, about the smell in a room she never entered without knocking.


Dandy Brook Pharmacy is not a retail chain. It occupies the ground floor of a three-story building on Banklick Street, one block south of the approach to the John A. Roebling Suspension Bridge. The Roebling Bridge connects Covington, Kentucky to Cincinnati, Ohio across the Ohio River. Its towers are stone — limestone and brick, in the Gothic Revival style, with vertical arch windows cut into each face. Two windows per tower. Four towers total. Eight windows. This is the bridge Henry Collins crossed on foot every working morning of his adult life in Covington — walking from the parking structure on the Cincinnati side, over the river, to the front door of Dandy Brook. Eight minutes. He took the same route. Marielle knows this because she occasionally saw him coming from the window above the counter, which faces the bridge's Kentucky approach.

The pharmacy occupies a long narrow space, deeper than it appears from the street. The retail counter is at the front. The compounding lab is at the back, separated from the retail area by a door with a small rectangular window in it, wire-reinforced glass. The lab is where the work happens — where raw active pharmaceutical ingredients, the bulk powders and crystalline compounds that arrive in sealed amber glass jars with certificates of analysis, are measured and combined and compounded into the specific formulations that a compounding pharmacy produces for patients whose needs fall outside the range of commercially manufactured medicines. It is exacting work. It requires precision and knowledge and a particular relationship with material — with the way substances behave at the molecular level, the way they interact with heat and moisture, the way they resist or accept combination.

Henry Collins had been doing this work for nine years.

He had a degree in pharmacy from the University of Kentucky. He had completed a compounding specialty certification in two thousand and twelve. He had a documented and resolved history with the Kentucky Board of Pharmacy — a prescription discrepancy in two thousand and sixteen, a sanction, a voluntary outpatient treatment program he completed in two thousand and seventeen. His pharmacy license had been reinstated. He had been clean, by all accounts, from mid-two thousand seventeen until some point in mid-two thousand nineteen, when the accounts became inconsistent with each other.

The pharmacist-owner of Dandy Brook, in the incident report filed with the Kentucky Board of Pharmacy on March third, two thousand and twenty, describes Henry as an excellent technician. The word is chosen carefully — excellent, not exceptional, not irreplaceable. Excellent in the way of someone whose work product can be evaluated and found good. The incident report is not an appreciation. It is a liability document. It reads like one.


The Certificate of Analysis arrives with every bulk API shipment. This is a legal requirement under the Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act. The certificate documents what is in the jar: identity, purity, potency, heavy metal content, microbial limits, moisture content, and the compound's physical characteristics — its appearance, its odor, its crystalline form, and its infrared absorption profile. The infrared profile is a kind of molecular fingerprint. Every compound absorbs infrared light at specific wavelengths determined by the bonds between its atoms, by the geometry of how it is put together. This fingerprint does not change. The same compound, synthesized by different manufacturers, tested in different laboratories, will produce the same absorption peak at the same wavelength, because the geometry is the geometry. Chemistry does not remember manufacturers. It only knows structure.

The compound that arrived at Dandy Brook in late two thousand and nineteen was a legitimate pharmaceutical ingredient. Its Certificate of Analysis was in order. It arrived in sealed amber glass jars — the standard amber glass that blocks ultraviolet light, that is used specifically to protect photosensitive compounds from degradation. The powder inside was white. Fine-grained. Crystalline, with the slightly faceted quality of a compound whose structure organizes itself into regular geometric patterns at the molecular level — the kind of structure that, under certain conditions, catches available light and returns it.

This is where Marielle's statement, on page seven, contains the detail she has no way to understand.

In the months before Henry disappeared, he developed a habit with the incoming shipments. When a new jar arrived — when the amber glass jar was uncrated and signed for and brought back to the lab — Henry would hold it up to the light before logging it. Not for a moment. For a long time. Long enough that Marielle noticed it from the doorway more than once, through the wire-reinforced window. She would see him standing under the fluorescent fixture, jar held at eye level, tilted slightly, turning it. He had handled hundreds of jars. He knew the procedure. She assumed he was checking for contamination — looking for discoloration, for moisture intrusion, for any sign that the seal had been compromised in transit. This is something pharmacists do. It is ordinary. It is caution. She did not think much of it.

The DEA field audit report, partially declassified under a Freedom of Information request, is dated March nineteenth, two thousand and twenty. The case number is redacted. The compound is referred to throughout as Compound A. The pharmacist is referred to as H.C. The document follows the format of a standard controlled substance audit — inventory reconciliation, log review, equipment inspection, specimen collection. In the inventory section, the auditors note an unaccounted quantity of approximately three hundred and forty grams of Compound A over a four-month period. Three hundred and forty grams. The log shows receipt. The log does not show the corresponding dispensing or disposal entries that would account for that quantity.

This is the inventory discrepancy. This is what the anonymous complaint to the pharmacy board, received three weeks before Henry disappeared, pointed toward. This is the crack the auditors were following.

In the equipment inspection section, the auditors note trace residue recovered from the ceramic mortar Henry used most often for blending operations. Standard protocol. The residue was collected and sent for spectroscopic analysis. The certificate of analysis for the incoming compound showed an infrared absorption peak within normal documented parameters for a compound of that class. The residue from the mortar showed something different. The auditors note, in the language of a report written by people who are describing something they do not have a category for, that the spectroscopic signature of the trace residue was anomalous. The term they use is anomalous. The field auditor's handwritten margin note, declassified with the rest of the document, is more specific.

The note reads: consistent with oscillation patterns outside documented pharmaceutical parameters — recommend referral.

The referral was never made. By March nineteenth, two thousand and twenty, Henry Collins had been gone for eighteen days.


What the archive knows about the last day:

The Dandy Brook Pharmacy incident report, filed by the pharmacist-owner, contains a brief description of how the compounding lab was found on the morning of March second, two thousand and twenty. Equipment clean. Surfaces wiped down. Instruments in their designated positions. The compounding log current through the previous evening, all entries in Henry's handwriting, in the standard format — compound name, quantity, patient identifier or batch number, initials — until the final entry, which is dated March first, two thousand and twenty, entered at nine forty-seven in the evening.

The entry reads: inventory noted.

That is all it says. Not a compound name. Not a quantity. Not a batch number. No initials. The pharmacist-owner describes Henry's handwriting in that entry as unusually steady. She does not expand on this. The report moves to the next numbered field.

Inventory noted. In a log designed to track the movement of controlled substances from receipt to disposal, in a format that requires specificity by regulation, the final entry is two words that function as a kind of notation for completion. Not what was noted. Not what the inventory revealed. Only that the noting had occurred, and that it was done.


Marielle Ostrowski's pharmacy board statement ends on page eleven with a passage that is separated from the preceding text by a line break, as if she paused before writing it.

She is describing the last time she saw Henry.

It was March first, two thousand and twenty, she says. A Sunday. She had come in briefly to handle a Saturday prescription pickup she had missed the day before. Henry was there, which surprised her — she had not expected to see him. He was in the lab. The door with the wire-reinforced window was closed. She knocked. He did not answer, but she could hear the mortar. She left the prescription on the counter and gathered her things to leave.

She was at the door when she turned back.

He was standing in the doorway of the compounding lab. Just standing there, one hand on the doorframe, looking back into the lab. Not at her. Not at the door. Back into the room he had just come from. She says she had the impression that he was not doing this consciously — that something had made him stop before he could move forward, and the something was behind him, in the lab, not ahead of him. She waited a moment. He did not turn around. She said goodbye. She is not sure he heard her.

Then he left. He walked past her without speaking. She heard the front door close.

Her last line in the statement reads: I thought he forgot something.


Henry Collins was declared presumed dead by a Kenton County judge in October of two thousand and twenty, seven months after he stopped being findable. The cause of death on the certificate, issued without a body, under Kentucky statute for presumed death, is listed as probable drug overdose. His ex-wife Margaret Collins and their two children were notified. His pharmacy license was administratively terminated. His personal effects, boxed by the landlord when the lease lapsed and the rent stopped being paid, were given to his ex-wife. The DEA audit report was filed and the investigation closed. Dandy Brook Pharmacy, under new compounding staff, continued to operate.

The record, as of March two thousand and twenty, contains: one person missing. One spectroscopic anomaly. One final log entry in unusually steady handwriting. One pharmacy technician who thought he forgot something.

What the record does not contain: Henry Collins.

What the record cannot account for: the distance between the Certificate of Analysis and the residue reading. Between what the jar said the compound was and what the compound, as Henry had been working with it for four months, had become in practice. Between the frequency the document describes and the frequency the mortar contained. Between the man who crossed the Roebling Bridge every morning and whatever it was he was looking for when he held the jars up to the fluorescent light.

The archive is not claiming Henry Collins is alive. The archive is not claiming he died. The archive is noting only what the official explanation cannot account for — the gap between the two spectroscopic readings, the referral that was never made, the two words at the end of a log that was designed to hold much more. And the question that follows from that gap: if the compound Henry Collins was working with was not the compound on the certificate, then what was it? Where did it resonate? And where does that frequency lead?

The archive does not know. What the archive knows is that a field auditor — a trained federal inspector working a routine controlled substance audit in a compounding pharmacy in Covington, Kentucky — held a ceramic mortar under a spectroscope and wrote in the margin of his report that what he was measuring was consistent with oscillation patterns outside documented pharmaceutical parameters. He wrote the word referral. He did not write what agency he was referring to, or what system he expected to receive that referral and know what to do with it.

He did not need to write it. He knew. He wrote recommend referral and set down his pen and at some point between March nineteenth and whenever the investigation was formally closed, the recommendation was reviewed and the investigation was closed without it.

The archive is not claiming this means anything.

The archive is only noting that between the oscillation pattern outside documented pharmaceutical parameters and the closed investigation lies the same distance Henry Collins traveled in four months of standing under a fluorescent light, holding an amber glass jar up to see what came back.

Something came back. The Certificate of Analysis described one geometry. The mortar held another. What happened between those two readings is what the archive opens.

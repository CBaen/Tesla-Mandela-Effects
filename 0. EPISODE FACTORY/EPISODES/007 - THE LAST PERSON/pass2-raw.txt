The record of Henry Collins's marriage begins with a photograph.

It is not in any official document. It is not in the coroner's inquest file, not in the DEA audit report, not in the pharmacy board's sanction records. It exists in a box of personal effects that the landlord of Henry's apartment building on Scott Boulevard packed and sealed and gave to Margaret Collins in the fall of two thousand and twenty, after the lease lapsed and the rent stopped and the judge in Kenton County signed the presumed death certificate. Margaret describes the box in her deposition testimony. She describes it the way someone describes an object they have decided not to open again: with the precision of someone who looked once, carefully, and then closed the lid.

The photograph is in a silver frame. Not the original silver frame from their wedding — that one was in her house in Florence, Kentucky, where it had been since the divorce was finalized in two thousand and eighteen. This frame was one Henry had bought separately, later. He had printed a copy of the photograph and put it in a new frame and kept it on the windowsill of an apartment he lived in alone for a year and a half before he disappeared. The photograph shows Margaret and Henry at the Cincinnati Museum of Art, sometime in their first year together. Henry is laughing at something out of frame. Margaret is looking at him.

She sees herself in the photograph looking at him and understands, she tells the coroner, that she had been trying to solve him since before they were married. That she had always assumed the solving was a temporary condition — that eventually the mechanism of him would become legible and she could stop. She had not stopped. She does not say this with bitterness. She says it the way someone reports a fact about weather: with the neutrality of a person who has made their peace with the climate.

Henry Collins and Margaret Brauer were married in two thousand and six. He was twenty-eight. She was twenty-six. They met in graduate school — he was finishing his pharmacy degree at the University of Kentucky, she was completing a master's in library science at the same institution, and they occupied the same table in the science library on three consecutive Tuesday evenings before either of them said anything to the other. She was researching pharmaceutical reference databases for a cataloging project. He was studying for his pharmacokinetics examination. She asked to borrow his highlighter. He gave her the entire case.

Two children: Daniel, born in two thousand and eight, and Claire, born in two thousand and eleven. The family lived in Florence, Kentucky — the Boone County side of the greater Cincinnati area, suburban and quiet, fifteen minutes from Covington. Henry drove to work. Then, for the last three years of their marriage, he drove to Covington and parked in the Cincinnati-side garage and walked the bridge. Margaret does not know when he started walking. She knows it was after the children were in school. She knows because he came home later than he used to, and when she asked, he told her about the bridge.

He had started noticing the towers, he said. The Gothic windows. He said he found them legible in a way that architecture rarely was for him — that there was something in the proportion of the stone relative to the opening that settled something, though he couldn't say what. Margaret describes this conversation in her deposition. She says she thought it was just a phase. A lot of people go through phases with architecture, she says, when their lives need something to hold onto.

The deposition is a document of a woman being asked to translate her marriage into administrative language. The coroner's questions are thorough and flat. Margaret's answers are careful. She is not performing grief for the record. She is completing a task that grief requires of her — the task of transforming a person into a case. She does it well. She is a librarian by training. She knows how to organize knowledge that costs her something to hold.

The two thousand and sixteen sanction is in the Kentucky Board of Pharmacy public records. The case number is a public document. The findings are a public document. They describe a prescription discrepancy at a previous employer — a compounding pharmacy in Newport, Kentucky, where Henry had worked before Dandy Brook — in which a controlled substance, a benzodiazepine, had been compounded and dispensed under a legitimate patient's name but not delivered to that patient. The quantity was small. The investigation could not determine definitively whether the quantity had been diverted for Henry's personal use or whether it had been a record-keeping error of the kind that sometimes arises in high-volume compounding operations. The board found insufficient evidence for the higher charge. They found sufficient evidence for the sanction.

The sanction suspended his license for forty-five days. It required completion of an approved outpatient substance use treatment program. It required twelve months of supervised practice. It required random drug testing for eighteen months following reinstatement.

He completed everything. His license was reinstated in the spring of two thousand and seventeen. He joined Dandy Brook Pharmacy in August of that year, passing the background check required for hire at a controlled substance facility with a disclosed history and a completed treatment record. He was, by all supervising pharmacist reports from the eighteen months of supervised practice, excellent. The word again. Excellent. The word that describes a performance that satisfies without illuminating.

Margaret knew about the sanction before it happened. She says this in her deposition without being asked — volunteers it, in a way that suggests she wants it on record that she was not deceived. She knew. She had known for longer than the pharmacy board had known. She had known the way you know about weather that's been building for a season — the subtle shifts in pressure, the quality of the light on mornings that seem normal, the way a person starts handling certain conversations with a kind of carefulness that wasn't there before. She had known and she had said something and Henry had listened and then the knowing hadn't changed anything.

She is not angry. She is, in the deposition testimony, somewhere past anger — in the territory that opens up on the other side of anger after someone you love has made a choice you cannot follow, and you have forgiven the choice and continued, and then the choice was made again.

The outpatient treatment program ran from October of two thousand and sixteen through March of two thousand and seventeen. Henry attended twice weekly. He completed all required components. He was discharged from the program with a positive assessment from the clinical director. He was, by his own account and by Margaret's account in the deposition, sober from the completion of the program through mid-two thousand and nineteen. Two years and some months. She describes this period as one in which he was present in a way he had sometimes not been before. More available for the children's schedules. More willing to let the evenings be quiet without filling them. She describes a weekend the four of them spent at the Daniel Boone National Forest in the summer of two thousand and eighteen. He was, she says, entirely there.

They divorced in two thousand and eighteen, that same fall, six weeks after the national forest trip.

She does not explain this. The coroner does not ask her to. The archive notes it without framing — the way a document notes a date, without offering the feeling of the interval between dates. What the deposition contains is not the explanation for the divorce. What it contains is the aftermath: two people who chose different configurations for the people they were trying to become, and the children who moved between those configurations as children do, which is to say: adapting, without being asked to, without being able to say what the adaptation costs.

Henry's apartment on Scott Boulevard was, by all accounts, spare. The landlord, who provides a brief statement attached to the lease termination records, describes it as very clean and not very lived-in. The furniture was standard rental furniture. He had added nothing to it except the things he had arrived with from the Florence house: some books, some kitchen equipment, a worktable he kept in the second bedroom, which the landlord understood to be a home workspace.

What the landlord did not know until the box was packed was what the worktable held.

Margaret describes the contents of the second bedroom in her deposition, because the coroner asks about the apartment's condition and whether there was anything unusual among his effects. She pauses before answering this question. The transcript records a pause of five seconds. Then she says: the popsicle sticks.

Henry had been building with popsicle sticks. Not as a craft project. Not as something for the children — the children had not visited the apartment more than twice, and neither visit involved the second bedroom. He had been building structures. Architectural structures, in the sense that they were designed to represent something rather than simply assembled: frames, towers, vaulted sections, arched openings made from sticks glued at careful angles. The worktable held four completed structures and one in-process one, along with a supply of craft sticks in a flat box, a bottle of wood glue, and a set of small clamps. There were drawings as well — pencil sketches on graph paper, showing plans and elevations for structures that did not correspond to any recognizable building. But they corresponded to each other. The proportions recurred across the drawings. The ratio between horizontal and vertical members repeated with a precision that suggested it was not accidental.

Margaret looked at the drawings for a long time in the apartment before she packed them. She is precise about this in her deposition, in the way she is precise about all of it — not because precision is her shield, but because precision is her mode. She looked at the drawings and she understood that Henry had been working on these for a long time, and that no one had seen him working on them, and that the work had required something from him. She could see it in the quality of the glue joints, in the way the clamps had left slight marks in the wood where he had applied them to let a joint cure overnight. This was not the kind of work that gets done in spare minutes. This was the kind of work that gets done when someone has found something to do with their hands while their mind goes somewhere else.

She packed the structures carefully. She packed the drawings. She does not know where they are now. She thinks they are in her basement.

She thinks they are in her basement with the same flat neutrality with which she says everything else. The deposition is not a document of someone who has found equilibrium. It is a document of someone who has gone so far through the loss of equilibrium that they have arrived somewhere beyond it — some territory that is not peace but is adjacent to peace, in the way a field adjacent to a forest contains some of the forest's darkness without being the forest.

The relapse is the hardest part of the deposition to read, because Margaret does not describe a relapse. She describes a series of small changes, none of which would, alone, constitute evidence of anything. A change in the rhythm of his texts responding to Daniel's and Claire's school schedules. A quality in his voice on the phone — not a different quality, she says, more like a subtraction. Something that had been in his voice during the two good years that was no longer consistently present. She describes a visit in August of two thousand and nineteen, when he took the children to a Reds game in Cincinnati and brought them home two hours later than expected. The children said he had seemed fine. They said he had watched the game and bought them food and walked them back over the bridge. She says she does not know whether anything happened at the game, or whether August of two thousand and nineteen is even the right frame. She says she does not know when it started again. She says: I know when I stopped being sure it hadn't.

She stopped being sure it hadn't in October of two thousand and nineteen.

The archive notes: October of two thousand and nineteen is the same month the compound arrived at Dandy Brook Pharmacy.

The Certificate of Analysis for the first shipment of Compound A is dated October seventh, two thousand and nineteen. The receipt log at Dandy Brook records it arriving on October eleventh. The pharmacy board's timeline, reconstructed during the audit, places Henry's first documented personal use of the compound — inferred from inventory reconciliation, not from any direct evidence — sometime in the second half of October.

The archive is not suggesting causation. The archive is noting proximity. It is the nature of the archive to note proximity.

The DEA field audit report's spectroscopic section occupies pages twelve through fifteen of the nineteen-page document. Most of it is standard — the protocol for residue collection, the chain of custody for the specimens, the laboratory that performed the analysis. The laboratory is a contracted facility in Columbus, Ohio, one of three facilities the DEA field office uses for this type of analysis. The laboratory report, attached as Exhibit C to the audit, is thirteen pages and runs through the full spectroscopic workup with the specificity required for a federal record.

Eleven of those thirteen pages are consistent with what you would expect to find in a compounding pharmacy's residue analysis. The residue from the blending spatulas, from the powder balance trays, from the graduated cylinders — all of it consistent with the compound on the certificate, all of it absorbed at the expected frequencies, all of it within the range that the Certificate of Analysis documents. Compound A, as it existed in the equipment Henry was not using most often, was the compound on the label.

The ceramic mortar is different.

Pages twelve and thirteen of the laboratory report describe the spectroscopic analysis of the residue recovered from the mortar — the forty-millimeter pharmaceutical-grade ceramic mortar that Marielle Ostrowski describes in her statement as the one Henry used for blending operations, the one whose sound she could hear from the counter, the sound she describes as the sound of someone doing something that required everything he had. The laboratory technician who performed the analysis documents, in the flat language of someone recording an unexpected finding in a standard dataset, that the infrared absorption profile of the residue from this specific mortar shows a primary peak that does not correspond to the documented absorption peaks for Compound A. It does not correspond to known degradation products. It does not correspond to common contaminants. The peak sits at a wavelength the report flags as outside the standard reference library for this compound class.

The laboratory technician does not interpret this finding. Interpretation is not their job. They flag it. They forward it.

The field auditor interprets it. Or begins to. The handwritten margin note on page twelve reads: consistent with oscillation patterns outside documented pharmaceutical parameters. Below that, three words: recommend referral.

The note does not say what oscillation patterns. It does not say what parameters are being departed from, or toward what. It does not say what agency the referral is addressed to, or what classification system that agency operates under that would have a category for this finding. It says only that the finding exceeds what this report is designed to contain, and that the exceeding should go somewhere.

The document does not say where somewhere is.

It says the referral was recommended. The investigation closed without it. The archive does not have access to what a referral, had it been made, would have reached. The archive only notes that a trained federal inspector encountered a residue reading in a pharmacy in Covington, Kentucky, and understood, without being able to say so in language the document could hold, that the reading pointed somewhere outside the jurisdiction of the document.

The compound arrived as one thing. In the mortar it became another thing. Between those two states lay four months of Henry Collins holding amber glass jars up to fluorescent light, learning to distinguish geometries by sight. Four months of standing under the fixture in the compounding room, tilting the jar, watching the way the crystalline powder inside caught and returned the light — each jar returning it slightly differently, the molecular structure of the compound expressing itself in the pattern of refraction, the way any crystalline solid expresses its internal geometry through the way it handles light that passes through or across it.

A crystalline compound is not a passive object. It has a structure — a regular, repeating internal arrangement of atoms that determines how it interacts with energy of every kind: light, heat, mechanical force, vibration. The geometry is not decorative. It is the compound's identity. Change the geometry and you have a different compound. The Certificate of Analysis documents the geometry by its infrared signature, because infrared light interacts with molecular bonds in a way that is both specific and stable. The same geometry always produces the same infrared profile. This is the pharmacist's guarantee: what the certificate says the compound is, the compound is, and the infrared test is the proof.

But Henry had been working with this compound in the mortar, every day, for four months. He had been grinding it. He had been holding it under pressure — the specific, patient, rhythmic pressure of the ceramic pestle against the ceramic bowl, the kind of pressure that Marielle's statement describes as having its own sound, a sound with more regularity to it, more patience. And what happens to a crystalline compound under sustained mechanical pressure is a question that the pharmaceutical industry has studied in the context of stability testing, because crystalline form matters for bioavailability and shelf life, and mechanical stress can, under the right conditions, induce what is called a polymorphic transformation — a rearrangement of the crystal's internal geometry without any change in its chemical composition. Same atoms. Different architecture.

The archive does not know if this is what happened.

The archive knows that the compound on the certificate and the compound in the mortar had different infrared signatures. The archive knows that the field auditor, who would have been familiar with polymorphic transformation as an explanation, did not write polymorphic transformation in his margin note. He wrote something else. He wrote oscillation patterns outside documented pharmaceutical parameters, which is a phrase that belongs to a different vocabulary than pharmaceutical stability science. It is a phrase that belongs to the vocabulary of frequency — of resonance, of objects responding to energy delivered at specific rates.

Nikola Tesla understood resonance before the vocabulary existed to describe what he understood.

The electro-mechanical oscillator he patented in eighteen ninety-three and eighteen ninety-four was a device for delivering energy at precise, controllable frequencies. The principle was simple in the way that all foundational principles are simple when stated directly: every physical structure has a natural frequency. A frequency at which it wants to move. A frequency at which the energy you deliver to it is not absorbed but amplified — returned to you, multiplied, because the structure is not resisting the energy, it is joining it. Tesla attached an early version of this device to a steel support beam at his laboratory at forty-six East Houston Street in New York City. He tuned it. He tuned the frequency of the oscillations the device was producing until they matched the natural frequency of the beam — the rate at which the beam, if struck and left alone, would prefer to vibrate. When he found the match, the beam began to amplify. Not to vibrate more — to join the frequency, to contribute its own energy to the oscillation, to become part of the source rather than remaining the target.

He described what followed to the Brooklyn Eagle, in nineteen thirty-five, as heavy machinery flying around. He said the neighborhood had begun to shake. He said he destroyed the oscillator with a sledgehammer.

The principle he had discovered was not that resonance was dangerous. The principle was that it was real — that every physical structure contained within itself a frequency at which it would respond completely, at which the boundary between structure and vibration would dissolve, at which what the structure was made of would participate in what was being done to it rather than simply receiving it. The sledgehammer was not a rejection of the principle. The sledgehammer was the only instrument available for stopping it, given that the stopping was necessary.

What was happening in Henry Collins's ceramic mortar for four months, every working day, in a compounding lab one block from the Roebling Bridge's Kentucky approach, was something the DEA field audit report does not have a category for. The category it reaches toward — oscillation patterns outside documented pharmaceutical parameters — is not a pharmaceutical category. It is a physics category. It is the category Tesla was working in when he attached a device to a steel beam and found the frequency at which the beam would answer.

The compound, as Henry was working with it, was answering.

The archive does not know what frequency it was answering at. The archive knows only the delta: the distance between what the Certificate of Analysis documented and what the mortar contained. The Certificate of Analysis described one geometry. The mortar held another. Between those two geometries is the spectroscopic gap that the field auditor's margin note points toward and does not enter.

The archive is not claiming Henry Collins understood what was happening to the compound. The archive is noting that he behaved, for four months, as if he were learning to read it. As if the jars held information that was available only visually, in the pattern of light returning from the crystalline structure, and that he was developing the ability to distinguish one pattern from another. As if some jars were more like the mortar's reading and some were less, and he was selecting.

A man who had spent nine years working with crystalline compounds at the pharmaceutical scale, who had a specialty certification in compounding chemistry, who understood infrared absorption profiles as professionally as he understood anything — such a man would know that different shipments of the same compound could have slightly different crystalline forms depending on synthesis conditions. Would know that the geometry varies within acceptable range. Would know, if he had been watching the jars for four months with the specific attention Marielle describes, that the variation was visible in the way the powder caught the light.

He was looking for the jar with the reading that matched the mortar.

He was looking for the frequency.

He did not know what name to give what he was doing. The archive knows this because of what is not in the notebook. The notebook, which Margaret describes in her deposition, is mostly illegible — the handwriting compressed and small, the pages filled in a way that suggests writing under pressure, writing that was trying to keep up with something. She could read only three words, on the last page. She does not say what they were. She says she did not want them in the official record.

The archive does not know what the three words were. The archive notes only that a man who had spent four months doing something without a name for it arrived, at some point, at something he could write in three words and chose not to make public. Not because the words were shameful. The deposition makes this clear — Margaret is asked whether the words were distressing, and she says no. Not distressing. Not shameful. She says: they felt like something that belonged to him.

Three words that belonged to him. On the last page of a notebook otherwise illegible. Three words in the same handwriting that wrote inventory noted in the compounding log at nine forty-seven in the evening of March first, two thousand and twenty, in handwriting the pharmacy owner described as unusually steady.

There are photographs of Henry Collins throughout the box of effects. Margaret describes them in her deposition because the coroner asks about documentation and personal materials. The photographs are not unusual — Henry at family events, Henry with the children, a handful of older photographs from before the marriage. But there are also photographs he had printed and framed and placed around the apartment, and these are the ones she lingers on, because they are not of people.

They are of the bridge.

He had taken photographs of the Roebling Bridge from multiple angles over what appears to be several years — the pedestrian approach, the towers from below, the towers from the river. He had them in frames. Three of them on the mantle of the gas fireplace. One on the worktable in the second bedroom, propped against the wall where he could see it while he worked. Not as decoration. The coroner asks Margaret whether she would describe them as decorative, and she says no, and does not elaborate. They are not decorative in the way a landscape photograph is decorative. They are the way photographs are when someone keeps an image nearby because they are still working something out.

The Roebling Bridge's towers have two windows each — vertical Gothic arches, one above the other, cut into the limestone and brick. Two windows per tower, in each of the four faces of each of the two towers. This is what every photograph shows. This is what every engineering drawing shows. This is what every maintenance record, every survey, every piece of documentation about this bridge since its opening in eighteen sixty-six shows. Two windows per tower. The bridge has always had two windows per tower. This is not in question.

But something is in question. Something that the photographs in Henry's apartment hold — in the space between what the photographs document and what Henry saw when he was looking at them — something that the archive does not yet have language for, and that the archive will return to, and that Henry, in the margin of one of his notebook pages, which Margaret also describes, had drawn.

Three windows per tower. The center one slightly narrower than the other two.

Not invented. Not imagined. Drawn with the precise hand of someone recording an observation rather than illustrating a fantasy. Drawn in the same measured way he drew the architectural elevations for his popsicle stick structures — with attention to proportion, with the slight correction marks of someone checking their work. He had drawn the bridge the way he saw it. And the bridge he saw had three windows.

The archive is noting this without interpretation. The archive does not interpret drawings. The archive describes what the drawings contain and what the record outside the drawings contains and leaves the distance between them for the listener to measure.

Margaret Collins finishes her deposition in one hour and forty-three minutes. The coroner thanks her. She collects her things. The transcript ends.

The archive does not know whether she looked at the bridge on the way home. The archive does not know what she sees when she looks at it.

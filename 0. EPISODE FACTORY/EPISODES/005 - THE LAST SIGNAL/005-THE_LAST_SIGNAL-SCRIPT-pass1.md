The static has a texture.

Not the flat white noise of a poorly grounded receiver — this is something finer, something with grain to it, the way the surface of old paper has grain when you press it between two fingers and drag them slowly across. The headphones are heavy. Not the polished consumer weight of something designed for comfort — the weight of iron and Bakelite and the wire mesh of a diaphragm built to work, not to please. They press against the skull at the temples and seal the ears completely, and what they seal you into is not silence.

The ship is still out there. The engine is still turning. The hull is still pushing through water at anchor, the small rocking motion of a large vessel in low Pacific swells. But the headphones have reassigned all of that — engine vibration, water against the hull, the creak of the vessel's frame responding to the pressure of its own weight — all of it reduced to a vibration that the body receives through the soles of the feet, through the seat of the chair, at a frequency below speech. The headphones have given you something else instead.

The central Pacific, as electricity.

Three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. The frequency that has been open all night. The dial is set. The log sheet is on the desk, and the pencil is resting in the hand, and the paper is slightly damp — not wet, not uncomfortable, but damp in the way everything on a Coast Guard cutter in the Pacific is slightly damp in the morning hours when the humidity has had all night to work. The pencil leaves marks that are a fraction softer than they would be on dry paper. The marks are sharper this way, somehow. More decisive.

The date in the log's upper margin: July second, nineteen thirty-seven.

The time: 0415 local.

The entries so far are routine. A position check. A weather notation. The kind of entries that exist in log books to prove that someone was paying attention — that the machinery was on, the watch was held, the frequency was monitored.

There is nothing yet on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz.

The static breathes. That is the only word for it. A trained ear hears the difference between dead static and a live channel — the quality of the noise changes with atmospheric conditions, with the angle of the ionosphere, with the particular geometry of signal paths across the Pacific on any given night. Right now the static is moving. Not words. Not signal. But the specific texture of a channel that the ionosphere is carrying, that has traveled ten thousand miles of atmospheric bounce already this morning and is still traveling. The static at this frequency at this hour feels — and the word is not imprecise — inhabited.

Then: a voice.

Through the grain of the static. Through the ten thousand miles of atmospheric travel. Through the Bakelite cups of the headphones pressed against your skull.

A woman's voice.

She is reporting. She is calm. She has been doing this for hours, and the calm is not ease — it is the specific composure of someone who has converted effort into discipline and has been running on discipline long enough that it no longer costs what it cost at the beginning. She gives coordinates. She gives an altitude. One thousand feet. She reports what she sees below her, which is ocean, and what she sees ahead of her, which is ocean, and what she cannot see, which is the island she is looking for.

The voice does not identify itself. The voice does not give a name. The voice gives information, and the information it gives is the information of someone who needs to be found.

The pencil moves. The log sheet receives the entry: 0742.

...KHAQQ calling Itasca. We must be on you but cannot see you but gas is running low. Been unable to reach you by radio. We are flying at one thousand feet.

Signal strength: S-five.

The strongest the equipment registers. The strongest a signal gets. The needle pinned.

He stands up.

The radioman stands up from the receiver desk in the radio room of the USCGC Itasca, and the headphones come off his head in one motion — the cups pulling away from his ears — and the ship comes back. All of it, at once. The engine's low continuous percussion. The water working against the hull. The ventilation system's rush and hiss. The sounds that the headphones had been keeping at bay for the past eight hours of watch are suddenly present again, not gradually but all at once, as if someone has opened a door between two rooms with different atmospheres and the pressure has equalized instantly.

Leo Bellarts runs.

Metal deck underfoot. The specific ring of boot soles on the companion-way of a Coast Guard cutter — not the dull thud of a wooden deck, not the soft absorption of carpet, but the ring of hard soles on painted steel, a sound that travels up through the legs and into the joints of anyone who has been running on ships for long enough to stop noticing it. The stairs up to the bridge: narrow, the angle steep, one hand on the rail, not for balance but out of the habit of ships. Through the hatchway.

The bridge. July second, nineteen thirty-seven. The central Pacific. The air on the open bridge of a cutter is not the same air as the radio room. The radio room is sealed and heated by the equipment and smells of warm electronics and brass fittings and the accumulated exhalations of men who have been awake for hours. The bridge is open, and the Pacific is right there — not the idea of the Pacific, but the fact of it. The air is salt and heat and the specific thin-chemical smell of the Itasca's stack smoke bending in the wind above the vessel.

The smoke column. He can see it, bending southeast, the signal fire they have been burning to make a visible smoke marker. Black-gray against the morning sky. Rising and bending.

He looks at the sky.

The signal at S-five means close. Maximum signal strength at this frequency, from this type of aircraft, from this type of transmitter — the range is fifty miles, maybe a hundred in ideal conditions. She is not across the Pacific from here. She is here. She is close enough that the signal is pinning the needle and she should be visible, coming in from the west-southwest, the silver shape of the Lockheed Electra descending toward Howland Island.

The sky is empty.

Not overcast — there are clouds, the morning cumulus of the equatorial Pacific, discrete and scattered, not a ceiling. Between the clouds: open sky. And in the open sky, no aircraft. The horizon in every direction: water. The smoke column from the Itasca's own stack. The low profile of Howland Island to the east — or what should be Howland Island, two miles of coral barely distinguishable from the surface of the sea.

No aircraft.

The strongest signal his receiver has logged in eight hours of watch is coming from a sky that contains nothing he can locate.

Leo Bellarts stands on the bridge of the Itasca and looks at the empty sky. The signal is still coming through on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. It was S-five when he left the radio room thirty seconds ago. It will be S-five when he goes back.

There is nothing in the sky.

He will carry this moment for the rest of his life. Not the disappearance — the moment before it. The sound of a voice at maximum strength. The empty sky. The forty-year gap between those two facts.

In the days after July second, there were other reports. Stations across the Pacific — and farther, along the American coasts — filed reception reports on or near three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. Broken transmissions. Voice fragments. Possible signals bearing toward the central Pacific. The Navy had assumed control of the search. The reports came in. They were assessed. They were filed.

Atmospheric interference. Ionospheric skip. Unconfirmed.

The investigation notes this. It returns to it.


The woman's voice belongs to Amelia Earhart.

This is not a secret and has never been. The call sign KHAQQ identifies her aircraft — the Lockheed Model ten-E Electra, registration NR sixteen-oh-two-oh, a twin-engine aluminum aircraft that she and her navigator have been flying across the Pacific for the past eighteen to twenty hours. The circumnavigation attempt. July, nineteen thirty-seven. She was trying to fly around the world.

It requires a moment — not commentary, but context — to understand what that meant in nineteen thirty-seven.

The first solo nonstop transatlantic flight had occurred ten years earlier. Commercial passenger aviation was a decade old and still a luxury. The Pacific crossing by air was a technical achievement of recent vintage — Pan American Airways had inaugurated its transpacific Clipper service only two years prior. The routes existed. The distances had been crossed. But they had been crossed with every possible advantage: with full ground crews, with relay stations, with navigational aids along the route, with the ability to land at intermediate points.

Earhart was crossing the Pacific with one navigator, one aircraft, and the radio on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz.

She had tried this once before. In March of nineteen thirty-seven, the same aircraft, the same route, a ground loop on takeoff in Honolulu had ended the attempt before it began — the plane damaged, the crew unhurt, but the plan reset. The second attempt began June first, nineteen thirty-seven, from Oakland, heading east: Miami, Puerto Rico, South America, Africa, India, Southeast Asia, Australia, New Guinea.

Lae, New Guinea. The last stop before Howland Island.

The leg from Lae to Howland is two thousand two hundred and forty-three nautical miles of open ocean. There is nothing between Lae and Howland. Not a landing strip, not an airfield, not a stretch of land large enough to set a twin-engine aircraft down. The Pacific, and then Howland Island, two miles long, half a mile wide, at its highest point ten feet above sea level. The target for a navigation shot from two thousand miles out.

On July second, nineteen thirty-seven, at approximately ten in the morning local time, the Electra took off from Lae.

The takeoff was nearly the end.

The Electra was loaded to maximum. One thousand one hundred gallons of aviation fuel in tanks that run through the fuselage — inside the aircraft, behind the firewall, in every cavity the designers had provisioned for them. The aircraft's gross takeoff weight at this loading was at or near the limit of what the airframe was certified for. The runway at Lae ran to the harbor edge — there was a grass strip, a slight downhill grade, and then the harbor, and then the Pacific. When the Electra lifted off, it did not climb immediately. It descended. The wheels left the ground and the aircraft sank toward the water, heavy with fuel and the physics of a wing not yet generating sufficient lift at the speed it had achieved at the runway's end.

Six feet. The Electra descended to within six feet of the harbor surface before the airspeed built enough for the wing to assert itself against the weight.

Six feet of clearance between the landing gear and the Pacific Ocean, in the first thirty seconds of a twenty-hour flight.

Then it climbed. Slowly. Settling into the altitude that would be its home for the next eighteen hours: one thousand feet above the water, low enough to stay below the high-altitude overcast, high enough to have some margin. Two Pratt and Whitney Wasp radial engines, each delivering four hundred and fifty horsepower, pushing the aircraft at a cruise speed of approximately one hundred and fifty miles per hour across an ocean that offered no reference points and no forgiveness.

That is the aircraft. That is the pilot. The man in the back of the aircraft is the one the popular account consistently forgets.


Frederick Joseph Noonan. Born April fourth, eighteen ninety-three, in Cook County, Illinois. Forty-four years old in the summer of nineteen thirty-seven. He sits behind a wooden bulkhead that divides the Electra's fuselage — the cockpit forward, the navigation space aft — and he cannot see Earhart and she cannot see him except through a small gap in the partition. Communication happens by note, passed forward through the gap, or by shouting over two Pratt and Whitney radials at cruise power.

He was the most experienced Pacific aerial navigator alive.

This is not hyperbole. Pan American Airways hired Noonan in nineteen thirty to help chart the transpacific Clipper routes — Midway, Wake Island, Guam, the Philippines, Hong Kong. He flew the survey missions. He made the measurements. He established the waypoint positions that the Clipper service would follow for the next decade. He knew the Pacific not from maps but from the specific experience of finding small islands in large oceans using the instruments available to a navigator in the nineteen thirties: a bubble sextant, a chronometer, a drift meter, and the sun.

At the end of nineteen thirty-six, Pan American let him go.

The company archive interviews cite drinking. The precise circumstances are not reconstructed here. What is established: Noonan was available when Earhart needed a navigator for the world flight, he took the job, and on March twenty-seventh, nineteen thirty-seven — two months and six days before the Electra disappeared — he married Mary Beatrice Martinelli in Yuba City, California.

Two months married.

He is sitting behind the bulkhead. His job is to tell Earhart where they are, which requires first determining where they are, which in the open Pacific in nineteen thirty-seven means: taking a sun shot with the bubble sextant when the sun is visible, running the celestial mathematics to determine a line of position, comparing that line to the dead reckoning track, adjusting the estimate. Doing this repeatedly, every hour or two, across eighteen hours of flight. Handing the result forward through the gap in the partition. Earhart adjusting the heading accordingly.

The procedure works when the sky is clear. The sky has not been entirely clear.

The Electra has been flying since ten in the morning, Lae time. It is now the early hours of July second, nineteen thirty-seven, ship time aboard the Itasca — which means it is already July second across the International Date Line, which Earhart crossed during the night. She has been in the air for what is now approximately fifteen to sixteen hours. The engines have been running for fifteen to sixteen hours. The fuel load — one thousand one hundred gallons at departure — has been decreasing for fifteen to sixteen hours.

The gauges on the instrument panel are showing numbers that get worse as the flight continues.


Close your eyes.

Not a request — a necessity. The radio room of the Itasca is gone. Bellarts is gone. The log is gone. What remains is the interior of the Electra, and you are in it.

The engines arrive first. Not the sound of engines heard from outside — the sound of engines when you are inside the aircraft they are attached to. Two Pratt and Whitney radials at cruise power produce a specific chord — not a single note, not a roar, but a complex mechanical harmony that enters through every surface simultaneously. Through the seat. Through the rudder pedals. Through the control column under the hands. Through the soles of the shoes pressed against the floor. The engines are not heard so much as they are absorbed. After fifteen hours, this frequency has become the frequency of the world. The body has reorganized itself around it. The quiet, at this point, would be the shock.

The instrument panel glows. Dials in darkness. The altimeter reading one thousand feet. The airspeed indicator — one hundred and forty-five miles per hour, slightly below cruise, the aircraft running lean to preserve fuel. The fuel gauges. The fuel gauges are the ones you return to. They are the ones that have been drifting leftward since Lae, the needles moving so slowly that any single observation reveals nothing, but the trend across hours is clear. The tanks are draining.

Below the aircraft: nothing. Not nothing in the way that the surface of a city at night is nothing — there are lights down there, there is evidence of human presence, the planet announcing itself. Nothing in the way that the Pacific announces itself: darkness absolute, and darkness, and the particular absence of any feature that would tell a human eye where the water ends and the sky begins. The stars have gone behind cloud. The horizon does not exist in any practical sense. The artificial horizon on the instrument panel — the gyroscopic instrument that substitutes for the absent real horizon — is the only evidence the aircraft is level.

You are in the most precisely instrumented aircraft Amelia Earhart could obtain, in the hands of the most experienced Pacific navigator alive, and you are flying through the specific darkness that exists when there is nothing below and nothing above and nothing to either side except the Pacific, which has no edges and no markers and offers only this: a surface two thousand miles from where you started that you cannot see from one thousand feet in the dark.

A note comes through the gap in the partition.

Not a voice. Noonan communicates by note because the engines are too loud for conversation through a wooden partition. His handwriting — and this matters, this is a specific thing, not a decoration — is steady. Eighteen hours of turbulence and the vibration of two engines and the cold at altitude have done nothing to the handwriting. The hand that produced the celestial calculations on the charts in the navigation space aft has the quality of a hand that has been trained to remain steady because unsteady hands produce numbers that end flights.

The note gives a sun line. A line of position, calculated from the last sun shot. Two numbers: one hundred fifty-seven degrees and three hundred thirty-seven degrees. The aircraft is somewhere on that line. The line runs through the Pacific like a thread — you are on the thread, somewhere, but the thread is long and Howland Island is a bead on the thread the size of nothing.

The sun line is all he has.

The time is approximately 0712, Itasca ship time. Howland Island should be visible from one thousand feet, if the clouds cooperate, if the haze is not too thick, if the morning angle of the sun is not throwing the coral flat into shadow that makes it vanish against the water. Two miles of island, ten feet high, against the surface of a sea that has been colored by overcast to a shade not meaningfully different from coral at distance.

The horizon is empty.

Water. Cloud shadow on water. The smoke column from the Itasca's stack — visible, according to the plan, from twenty miles or more — which should be directly ahead, which should be resolving into a smudge on the horizon by now. Ahead: water. To the left: water. To the right: water. Behind the aircraft, to the west-southwest, Lae and New Guinea have been below the horizon for seventeen hours.

The fuel gauges.

The fuel gauges are the clocks in this aircraft. They are measuring the same passage of time as the clock on the instrument panel, but in different units — not hours and minutes, but the irreversible mathematics of how long a Pratt and Whitney Wasp radial can run on what remains in the tanks. The calculation does not get better. It runs in one direction. It has been running in that direction for seventeen hours.

Howland Island is two miles long.

Ten feet high.

She cannot hear them.

The Itasca has been transmitting on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. The Itasca has been transmitting on schedule, on the agreed frequency, at full power. The signal is going out. There is no equipment failure in the log. The transmitters are operating. The frequency is clear.

She is transmitting at S-five — the maximum signal strength the equipment registers. She is close enough that the signal is pinning the needle. She is transmitting from somewhere that cannot be more than a hundred miles away.

This is the asymmetry. Not equipment failure — something more specific than that. A channel that carries signal in one direction and not the other. A frequency that works for transmission and not for reception. She is loud. She is clear. She is saying she cannot hear the station that is hearing her perfectly.

The radioman on the Itasca is Leo Bellarts. His name appears in the log's authentication. He has been awake since before the watch began. He is listening to a woman's voice at maximum signal strength telling him she cannot hear him.

At 0800, a third transmission: ...KHAQQ calling Itasca. We received your signals but unable to get a minimum. Please take bearing on us and answer three one oh five with voice.

She received their signals. That is the first confirmation from her end that the Itasca's transmissions are reaching the aircraft — some of them, some of the time. But she cannot get a minimum, which means she tried to take a directional bearing on the Itasca's signal and could not determine the direction. The signal was present but too inconsistent for the direction-finding equipment to lock. She is asking the Itasca to answer her on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz, voice, so she can take a bearing on their voice transmission.

The Itasca answers. On three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. Voice. Full power.

No response.

He asked it not as a formal inquiry, not as a challenge, but as a man who had been listening to a frequency for hours and could not account for what the frequency was doing. He said: Why couldn't she hear me on three one oh five?

The question has not been fully answered.

The frequency at 0843: three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. A S-five signal. Then silence. Not the silence of a signal that has gotten progressively weaker — the signal was at maximum strength in the last transmission. Not the silence of equipment failure. The silence of a radio that simply stopped transmitting on this frequency.

Because she said she was switching.

Six thousand two hundred and ten kilohertz. That was the frequency she named before she stopped. She was going to repeat the message on six two one oh. She was going to transmit there.

The Itasca could not receive voice on six thousand two hundred and ten kilohertz. Their receivers covered the frequency. But the Itasca could not transmit voice on six two one oh — only Morse code. The ground equipment had been configured for three thousand one hundred and five. When she moved to six two one oh, the channel she moved to was one the Itasca could hear but could not answer in the mode she was using.

She moved to a frequency they could not follow.

She has been gone for six hours already when O'Hare asks his question. She is not going to answer it.

The static on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz has the specific quality that O'Hare will try, later, in a private account that no official record will contain, to describe. Not the absence of a signal. The presence of a channel that should have a signal in it and doesn't. He will write, in the July second entry of a diary kept in his seabag during the operation: The static sounds different when you're waiting for someone. It has a shape. The shape of the voice that should be in it.

He is a twenty-three-year-old radioman on a Coast Guard cutter in the central Pacific. He has been listening to radio static for four years. He knows the difference between a dead channel and a live one. He knows the difference between atmospheric noise and signal-shaped absence.

The frequency has the shape of the voice that should be in it.

He will monitor three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz off-shift, on his personal equipment, for the next two days. On the early morning of July fourth — 0300, the dead watch, the hours when ionospheric conditions favor long-distance propagation — he will hear something that does not resolve into words but has the quality of a word trying to form. He will file a report. The duty officer will log it as atmospheric skip. O'Hare writes in the July fourth entry: It was not skip. I have been listening to skip for four years. Skip does not say words.

The duty officer's log will categorize the report. The search will go on.

The search will find nothing.


Here is what the official record established about the disappearance of Amelia Earhart and Frederick Noonan, and why it is the right place to begin before it is the wrong place to stop.

The aircraft was heavy. One thousand one hundred gallons of fuel is a great deal of fuel, but a Pratt and Whitney Wasp radial engine at cruise power burns approximately twelve to fourteen gallons per hour per engine. Two engines. Eighteen to twenty hours of flight time to Howland. The mathematics allow almost no margin. With favorable winds, the margin is sufficient. With headwinds — and the weather on the final leg from Lae included winds that pushed against the aircraft's track — the margin narrows to hours. Then to an hour. Then to minutes.

She was at one thousand feet when she transmitted at 0742. She had been airborne for approximately nineteen hours. She could not see Howland Island. She was circling — she said so, at 0758. Circling over a position she believed was correct, looking for an island that was not appearing on the horizon. The fuel that remained for circling was the fuel she was burning while circling. Every minute of the circle brought the gauges closer to the position that ends flights.

The ocean in that part of the Pacific is approximately four thousand meters deep. Two and a half miles, straight down.

The official position — the position of the United States Navy, of the Coast Guard, of every official inquiry that followed — is that Earhart ran out of fuel somewhere near Howland Island, attempted to ditch the aircraft in the water, and sank. The search that followed was the largest air-sea search in American history to that point: nine ships, sixty-six aircraft, approximately two hundred and fifty thousand square miles of ocean. Seventeen days. The USS Lexington alone contributed sixty-two aircraft to the effort. The estimated cost exceeded four million dollars in nineteen thirty-seven money.

They found nothing.

Not wreckage. Not fuel slick. Not emergency equipment. Not a trace of the aircraft or the two people inside it. The ocean at that depth, in that region, does not return what it takes. A twin-engine aluminum aircraft at two and a half miles of depth is not a recoverable object with the technology available in nineteen thirty-seven, or in the decades immediately following. The absence of wreckage does not prove the wreckage is not there. It proves the wreckage is not findable.

Leo Bellarts believed this. For decades, he believed it. In a letter written in nineteen sixty-one to Fred Goerner, a journalist and researcher who was investigating the disappearance, Bellarts wrote that Earhart was — peacefully on the bottom of the sea, no farther than one hundred miles from Howland.

He based this on the signal strength. S-five means close. If the signal was S-five, she was close. If she was close, and the search found nothing, she went down close. The ocean close to Howland is deep. The wreckage is there.

He carried this for the rest of his life.

He also carried the radio log. He had kept it — not because the Navy asked him to, not as part of any formal documentation requirement, but because he recognized, even in the immediate aftermath, that what the log contained was significant. The sequence of transmissions. The signal strengths. The asymmetry between what the Itasca heard and what the aircraft heard. He preserved it. Decades later, he donated it to the National Archives, where it is now held as part of the Leo G. Bellarts Papers, dated nineteen thirty-seven through nineteen seventy-one. Anyone who wants to read the last thing Amelia Earhart said to another human being can read it there.

The crash-and-sink theory is not a theory, in the pejorative sense. It is an assessment based on the available evidence: the fuel math, the empty sky, the search that found nothing, and the testimony of a man who was as close to the event as any living person. It is the conclusion a careful, experienced man reached after a lifetime of considering the evidence he had personally observed.

And it cannot account for the signals.

In the days following July second, nineteen thirty-seven, monitoring stations across the Pacific filed reception reports on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. Pan American Airways maintained radio listening stations across the Pacific as part of the infrastructure for its Clipper service — Midway, Wake Island, Honolulu, and others. These stations were staffed by trained radio operators who knew the difference between atmospheric interference and voice. They filed reports. Their logs indicate possible voice transmissions on or near three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz in the days after the disappearance.

A fifteen-year-old girl in Saint Petersburg, Florida, named Betty Klenck, was listening to her family's shortwave radio. She had the dial on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz. She heard a woman's voice, and she wrote in her notebook what she believed she heard. The notebook was not publicized for decades. It is now held by a research organization that has been investigating the disappearance for forty years. The notebook is legible. The entries are dated. The content of what she wrote has been examined by researchers who note that details she recorded — details she would have had no knowledge of in nineteen thirty-seven — are consistent with the known circumstances of the flight.

The Navy assessed the reports that came in during the search. Atmospheric interference. Ionospheric skip. Unconfirmed.

If the aircraft had gone down in deep water and sunk, those assessments are correct. A radio that is two and a half miles underwater does not transmit. Atmospheric skip and interference account for what was heard. The physics are clear.

The physics are also clear in the other direction.

If the aircraft was not on the ocean floor — if it was on land, on a reef, on a beach — a functional radio could transmit for days. The Electra's radio drew power from the aircraft's batteries. The batteries would last while the batteries lasted. The fuel in the tanks was not the fuel in the batteries. A landed aircraft, intact, with a functional radio and a pilot who knew how to operate it, could put a signal on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz for days.

Fifty-seven credible reception reports were logged in the days after July second. Credible in the sense that they came from trained operators, at stations with equipment capable of distinguishing voice from interference, in some cases with directional bearings pointing toward the central Pacific.

Pan American Airways' chief communications officer in San Francisco sent a routing memo to the Pacific station managers on July fifth, nineteen thirty-seven. The memo instructed all Pacific stations to discontinue monitoring three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz effective immediately. Reception reports from the prior seventy-two hours were to be filed as unconfirmed atmospheric phenomena. The memo noted that the Navy had assumed control of the search and that continued monitoring at company expense was not operationally justified. The stated reason is adequate and logical. A corporation does not continue expensive monitoring operations after the responsible government agency has assumed control of the relevant search.

The memo reads: Discontinue monitoring. File as unconfirmed atmospheric phenomena.

They were told to stop listening.

Someone was still transmitting from somewhere.

The crash-and-sink theory does not get smaller when the post-loss signals arrive. It gets insufficient. There is a difference. A theory that fails to explain an important fact has not been disproved — it may still be correct, it may be that the signals were atmospheric, it may be that fifty-seven trained operators were all wrong in the same direction at the same time. The theory remains.

But the signals do not go away.

They have been filed. They have been assessed. They have been attributed to atmospheric phenomena and moved to the appropriate category.

They are still there, in the record, in the logs of trained radio operators at stations spread across the Pacific.

A woman's voice on three thousand one hundred and five kilohertz, in the days after the silence.

Saying something.

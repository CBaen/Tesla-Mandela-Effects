**[CRITICAL] F1 — Vienna letter date: "seventeenth of November" is wrong**

Location: The paragraph beginning "The second letter that matters is dated the seventeenth of November, eighteen ninety-eight, written from the Hotel Krantz in Vienna."

What is wrong: Multiple sources — including the Mark Twain Project, Tesla Universe, and the Facebook post from the Mark Twain House citing the letter directly — date this letter as **November 8, 1898**, not November 17. The Brief also states "November 17, 1898 Vienna letter" which appears to be the error source. The letter date is a verifiable historical fact from a real document held in a real archive. A listener who looks it up will find November 8.

Correct fact: November 8, 1898.

Sources: Tesla Universe "Mark Twain and Nikola Tesla: Thunder and Lightning"; Mark Twain Project; Facebook post from Mark Twain House dated November 8, 1898.

Fix: Change "the seventeenth of November, eighteen ninety-eight" to "the eighth of November, eighteen ninety-eight" everywhere it appears or is implied.

---

**[IMPORTANT] F2 — "twenty-seven million people" applied to the Tesla-Westinghouse demonstration**

Location: The paragraph describing the World's Columbian Exposition: "the Westinghouse-Tesla system had lit a world's fair. The demonstration at the Court of Honor had converted the engineering establishment's skepticism… The alternating current polyphase system at the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago… proved alternating current to an audience of twenty-seven million people."

What is wrong: The 27 million figure is the total attendance for the entire fair across six months. The Westinghouse-Tesla electrical demonstration at the Court of Honor was one attraction among many. Saying Tesla "proved alternating current to an audience of twenty-seven million people" implies all 27 million witnessed the AC demonstration — they didn't. The figure is technically defensible as fair attendance but is applied as though it describes the reach of the Tesla demonstration specifically, which is an overstatement.

Correct fact: Total fair attendance was approximately 27.5 million (varying by counting method). The Westinghouse-Tesla demonstration lit the fair's buildings and was visible across the grounds, but "proved alternating current to an audience of twenty-seven million" overstates what happened. Better: "the Westinghouse-Tesla system had lit a world's fair that drew twenty-seven million visitors" — attributing the number to the fair, not the audience for the demonstration.

Sources: Wikipedia "World's Columbian Exposition"; Britannica.

### QUOTE ISSUES

**[CRITICAL] Q1 — Tesla pigeon quote is incomplete — possible truncation of the verified text**

Location: "He said: — 'I loved that pigeon as a man loves a woman, and she loved me.'"

What is wrong: Multiple sources confirm that O'Neill's *Prodigal Genius* (1944) records a longer statement. The commonly cited full quote is: *"I loved that pigeon as a man loves a woman, and she loved me. As long as I had her, there was a purpose to my life."* The script reproduces only the first sentence and stops. If the actual O'Neill text continues — and the evidence from secondary sources strongly suggests it does — then the script is presenting a partial quote as the complete quote, which is a Quote Rule violation (the quoted words are accurate, but the attribution implies this is the complete statement when it may not be).

What to do: Verify the exact stopping point of Tesla's statement in O'Neill's *Prodigal Genius* (1944), page references available in secondary sources. If the full quote includes the second sentence, either (a) include it, or (b) add a narrative bridge indicating Tesla continued before closing the quotation marks. Do not leave the truncated version as a direct quote if it misrepresents the full documented statement.

Note: The script's use of this quote is artistically powerful in its current form, and the first sentence is verified. The issue is whether the excerpt is presented as complete when it is not.

### ATTRIBUTION ISSUES

---

**[IMPORTANT] A1 — Johnson's journal: the Bancroft Library is named too specifically**

Location: "The altered archive locates it in the Johnson papers, in the section of private journals that the Bancroft Library holds in a collection that has not been fully digitized — a researcher would need to request physical access to reach it, and most researchers have not."

What is wrong: Johnson's papers at the Bancroft Library are real, confirmed, and searchable. Naming the Bancroft Library as the specific holding institution for a fabricated journal entry — even an undigitized one — attaches a fictional document to a real institution with a real finding aid. A determined researcher can request physical access to the Johnson Papers at the Bancroft and search for this entry. They will not find it. The attachment to a real archive with a real collection is exactly the condition the Production Guide warns against: "NEVER attach to a real archive, real catalog number, real journal, or real researcher that could be checked and found empty."

The Brief flagged this as "borderline" but the script reproduces the full Bancroft attribution without hedging. In audio, listeners will hear the Bancroft Library named as the specific home of this entry.

Recommended fix: Replace "the Bancroft Library holds" with a genericized description — "a collection of private journals held in a university library on the West Coast, in papers donated across several decades, not yet fully processed." This preserves the documentary texture without attaching the fabrication to a real searchable institution.

**[IMPORTANT] S1 — Narrator synthesizes fabricated source connections in one passage**

Location: The paragraph beginning "The narrator presents the letter without synthesis. The listener connects it to the January mornings at South Fifth Avenue and the fire and the train from Hartford and the calculations that were, however, complete."

Then immediately: "The connection event — the moment the listener assembles the picture themselves, the moment Clara's account of the space opening and Johnson's account of the agreement about each other and the Patent Office supervisor's annotation in a smaller hand all arrive at the same point in the listener's understanding — that event happens inside the listener, and it happens without the narrator's participation, and the narrator's silence at that moment is the most important thing the narrator does."

What is wrong: The narrator explicitly names the connection it is claiming not to make. By saying "the moment Clara's account… and Johnson's account… and the Patent Office supervisor's annotation… all arrive at the same point in the listener's understanding," the narrator is performing exactly the synthesis it claims to withhold. The narrator is telling the listener what sources to connect and in what way. The listener is supposed to do this; the narrator describing the listener doing it robs the event.

The Production Guide is explicit: "The narrator presenting each source in turn. The narrator does not synthesize. The narrator does not say 'and this confirms what the memo said.' The connection happens in the listener's mind."

Fix: Cut the second paragraph entirely. The first sentence ("The narrator presents the letter without synthesis") is itself meta-commentary that should also be cut. Let the letter end. Move directly to the next beat. The listener will do the work.

### DRAFTING ERRORS

**[MINOR] D1 — Closing section: near-identical phrasing appears twice in close proximity**

Location: Near the end of the script, these two passages appear within approximately 300 words of each other:

> "In the timeline the listener stands in, he was given one object for the full weight of what he was capable of feeling, and he gave it that weight without apology, and he described it in the most precise language available to him, and the language was: as a man loves a woman."

> "In the timeline you are standing in, Tesla spent two thousand dollars building a device for a pigeon's broken wing. He described the pigeon the way a man describes the person he needed most. He was not being metaphorical. He was giving you the most complete account he knew how to give."

Then again:

> "In another timeline, the record says he had someone who didn't need a substitute."

This final construction appears TWICE in near-identical form within two paragraphs of each other (once before "Which man received the better account of his own life?" and once the paragraph immediately preceding it). The repetition is semi-intentional — it reads like a drumbeat — but the second instance is so close to the first that it may flatten rather than deepen on TTS. Consider whether the intervening paragraph fully earns the repetition or whether the first instance should be trimmed.

---

**[MINOR] D2 — "the listener's own timeline" construction**

This phrase appears several times in the script and the Brief warns the narrator should not say "in the listener's timeline" — but the Brief's instruction was that the narrator of the ALTERED timeline should not flag that facts differ. The script actually handles this correctly — the "listener's timeline" language appears in passages that are analytically comparing archives, not in the narrator's voice from inside the altered timeline. No structural violation, but the frequency of the construction is worth reviewing for voice consistency in the final edit.
